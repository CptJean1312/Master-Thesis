Abstract

[P00026 | 436:1215 | NORMAL_TEXT]
Flood justice concerns not only where flood hazard occurs, but also how exposure, social vulnerability, and loss outcomes are distributed across places. This thesis examines these relationships at the municipal level in the German Elbe River Basin. The empirical study population is an RP500-defined flood corridor of 835 municipalities. Municipal flood exposure is derived from JRC river-flood hazard rasters for six return periods and refined at RP100 using DLR Land Cover DE to identify flooded Artificial Land. Socio-economic vulnerability is represented by a direction-coded, dimension-balanced index based on 23 BBSR INKAR indicators. A separate 5,000-year loss portfolio provides modelled loss occurrence, annual loss probability, and finite loss-return-period estimates.

The results do not show a positive corridor-wide vulnerability gradient in flood-area exposure. The correlation between the main vulnerability index and RP100 municipal flood share is approximately -0.05, and the relationship remains weak when exposure is restricted to Artificial Land. Modelled loss outcomes display a clearer, although still moderate and non-monotonic, social differentiation. Positive-loss municipalities are more common in the upper vulnerability groups, annual loss probability is positively associated with vulnerability (Spearman rho = 0.230), and finite loss-return-period estimates tend to be lower among more vulnerable municipalities within the positive-loss subset (Spearman rho = -0.198). 1.1 Motivation and Problem Framing

[P00039 | 2739:3529 | NORMAL_TEXT]
Flooding is among the most consequential environmental hazards of contemporary societies. As Jongman et al. (2012) note, flooding of coastal and fluvial systems affects several million people globally each year, while the population and value of assets located in flood-prone areas have increased substantially over recent decades. Their global assessment further suggests that economic exposure to river and coastal flooding has grown sharply and is likely to continue rising in the future. Flood risk is not only a matter of extreme hydrometeorological events. It is also a product of how populations, infrastructures, and economic assets have become increasingly concentrated in vulnerable locations. This makes flooding a problem of both physical hazard and socio-economic development.

[P00040 | 3529:3530 | NORMAL_TEXT]
⟦EMPTY PARAGRAPH⟧

[P00041 | 3530:4565 | NORMAL_TEXT]
In Europe, this problem has become especially visible in the context of climate change and adaptation. Rojas et al. (2013) describe river flooding as one of the major climate-related risks facing the European Union and show that future damages may rise dramatically if current levels of protection are simply kept constant. Their analysis projects expected annual flood damages in the EU from around EUR 5.5 billion under current conditions to almost EUR 98 billion by the 2080s in a no-adaptation trajectory, while also showing that adaptation can substantially reduce these losses and may be highly cost-effective. For the present thesis, this shifts the question from whether flood risk matters to how it is governed. Once adaptation and protection become public tasks, the spatial distribution of their benefits and limits becomes relevant. Flood risk management is not only a technical response to hazard. It is part of a broader political and social negotiation over future safety, acceptable losses, and territorial priorities.

[P00042 | 4565:4566 | NORMAL_TEXT]
⟦EMPTY PARAGRAPH⟧

[P00043 | 4566:5649 | NORMAL_TEXT]
Germany offers a particularly strong setting in which to examine these issues, because major flood events have repeatedly triggered policy change and public debate. Thieken et al. (2016) identify the 2002 flood as a decisive turning point in the German shift toward integrated flood risk management, while the 2013 event revealed both the continuing severity of flood losses and the role of institutional learning. Within this national context, the Elbe stands out as one of the most visible and relevant river corridors. Grabs (2016) shows that the extreme Elbe floods of 2002 and 2013 triggered the Elbe Flood Protection Action Plan and became a benchmark for broader debates on integrated flood risk reduction. The Elbe is not only a hydrological space, but also a historically significant corridor of settlement, intervention, and policy learning. It is precisely this combination of repeated flood experience, extensive protection efforts, and wider governance relevance that makes the Elbe a compelling case for studying the distribution of flood-related burdens and benefits.

[P00044 | 5649:5650 | NORMAL_TEXT]
⟦EMPTY PARAGRAPH⟧

[P00045 | 5650:6546 | NORMAL_TEXT]
Aggregate damage figures alone cannot capture the significance of flooding. Flood impacts are socially differentiated. Tovar Reaños (2021) shows for Germany that expected flood damages affect households differently across income groups and that welfare losses after floods disproportionately harm low-income households. More recent evidence by Odersky and Löffler (2024) points in the same direction from another angle. Using actual damage data from the 2021 floods in Germany, they show that flood exposure was regressive at the local level, with poorer neighbourhoods displaying substantially higher exposure than better-off nearby areas. These findings suggest that flood-related harm is not distributed randomly across space or society. Exposure, coping capacity, and recovery potential are shaped by socio-economic conditions, which gives flood governance a social distributional dimension.

[P00046 | 6546:6547 | NORMAL_TEXT]
⟦EMPTY PARAGRAPH⟧

[P00047 | 6547:7339 | NORMAL_TEXT]
This is the practical puzzle from which this thesis starts. If flood risk is growing, if adaptation and protection are becoming more important, and if flood-related harms are socially differentiated, the key issue is no longer only how much flood risk exists. It is also who is exposed, where losses occur, and whether these patterns align with broader socio-economic inequalities. The Elbe is particularly suitable for asking these questions because it combines a long history of flood events and protection strategies with marked spatial variation in local social conditions. The thesis therefore examines modelled exposure and loss outcomes as potentially uneven and justice-relevant spatial patterns. It does not treat those outcomes as a direct measurement of protective infrastructure.

[P00048 | 7339:7340 | NORMAL_TEXT]
⟦EMPTY PARAGRAPH⟧

[P00049 | 7340:8051 | NORMAL_TEXT]
Under climate change, this puzzle becomes even more pressing because adaptation decisions are not judged by efficiency alone. As de Goër de Herve (2022) and Thaler and Hartmann (2016) show, flood risk management also raises questions of fairness, because protection measures allocate public resources and shape who receives greater safety and who remains more exposed. Liao et al. (2019) further argue that flood prevention can generate environmental injustice when interventions redistribute floodwater rather than removing it altogether. The thesis approaches flood risk through a distributive justice lens, while the deeper conceptual discussion of environmental and flood justice is developed in Chapter 2.

1.2 Research Gap and Thesis Contribution

[P00052 | 8093:9378 | NORMAL_TEXT]
The literature on floods, vulnerability, and flood risk management is already substantial, and this thesis does not start from an empty field. A large body of work has examined hydrological hazard, damage estimation, governance reform, and the social dimensions of vulnerability. In parallel, a growing international literature has shown that flood exposure is often socially uneven rather than randomly distributed. Qiang (2019) demonstrates this for the United States at national scale, while Poussard et al. (2021) show that environmental inequalities in flood exposure are strongly shaped by the spatial scale at which they are measured. More recent work for Latin America reaches a similar conclusion and stresses that substantial social disparities in flood exposure can also be identified at the local level (Kephart et al., 2025). German research has also moved in this direction. Tovar Reaños (2021) links flood damages to inequality in welfare terms, and Odersky and Löffler (2024) provide evidence that flood exposure in Germany was regressive at the local scale during the 2021 event. These studies make clear that the relationship between flooding and social inequality is no longer a marginal topic. It has become an increasingly visible research field in its own right.

[P00053 | 9378:9379 | NORMAL_TEXT]
⟦EMPTY PARAGRAPH⟧

[P00054 | 9379:10825 | NORMAL_TEXT]
Still, an important gap remains. Existing studies often focus on one dimension in relative isolation: physical hazard and damage, social vulnerability measurement, or governance and protection strategies. These strands are not always brought together within one coherent distributive framework. This matters because flood inequality is, as Poussard et al. (2021) argue, a question of scale. Patterns that appear weak or invisible at broader regional levels can become much clearer when the analysis moves to smaller spatial units. Older German work already pointed to this problem. Fekete (2010) argued that an overview of social vulnerability to river floods in Germany was missing and emphasized that only few studies had addressed the non-structural and socially differentiated dimensions of flood risk in a systematic way. Rufat et al. (2015) add a broader methodological caution by showing that there remains a large gap between the contextual complexity of social vulnerability and the generalized indicator practices often used to measure it. Rufat et al. (2019) make a similar point by arguing that many social vulnerability models remain primarily descriptive. For the purposes of this thesis, the implication is not that previous work is inadequate. Rather, there is still limited municipality-level evidence that directly links flood exposure patterns with socio-economic vulnerability in a justice-oriented way for the Elbe corridor.

[P00055 | 10825:10826 | NORMAL_TEXT]
⟦EMPTY PARAGRAPH⟧

[P00056 | 10826:11765 | NORMAL_TEXT]
This is where the present thesis is positioned. Its contribution lies in combining municipality-based exposure analysis, a multidimensional socio-economic vulnerability index, and modelled loss outcomes within one framework. Empirically, it examines how flood exposure and simulated loss occurrence are distributed across municipalities with different social characteristics in the Elbe flood corridor. This responds to the scale problem identified in the literature and to the need for more spatially explicit evidence on distributive flood-risk patterns. The study does not measure protective infrastructure, although protection and residual risk remain important for interpreting why exposure and loss outcomes may diverge. The contribution is both empirical and conceptual: it adds municipality-level evidence for the Elbe corridor and situates it within debates on flood justice, socio-economic vulnerability, and spatial inequality.

2. Theoretical and Conceptual Background

[P00069 | 13567:13604 | HEADING_2]
2.1 Flood Risk Management in Germany

[P00070 | 13604:14876 | NORMAL_TEXT]
Flood risk management in Germany has changed substantially over the last decades. While earlier approaches were dominated by technical flood protection and the attempt to control water mainly through structural measures, more recent debates and policy developments increasingly frame flooding as a broader risk management problem. This shift was not purely conceptual, but strongly event-driven. According to Thieken et al. (2016), the 2002 flood marked an important reorientation toward integrated flood risk management in Germany. A similar point is made by Sairam et al. (2021), who note that the major events of 1993, 1995, and especially 2002 triggered policy decisions in favor of more systemic approaches. In this context, the European Floods Directive became a key institutional milestone, because it required member states to identify significant flood risks and prepare flood risk management plans. Germany implemented this broader orientation within its national legal and planning framework, which means that flood risk management today has to be understood as a combination of hazard assessment, spatial planning, emergency management, damage reduction, and long-term adaptation rather than as flood defense alone (Sairam et al., 2021; Thieken et al., 2016).

[P00071 | 14876:14877 | NORMAL_TEXT]
⟦EMPTY PARAGRAPH⟧

[P00072 | 14877:16005 | NORMAL_TEXT]
Major flood events make this shift easier to trace. Thieken et al. (2016) describe the 2002 flood as the most expensive natural hazard event in Germany up to that point, with damage of EUR 11.6 billion and 21 fatalities. The 2013 flood again caused very high losses, estimated at EUR 6 to 8 billion, but it also demonstrated that some improvements had already taken effect. Thieken et al. (2016) argue that by 2013 Germany had improved flood warnings, disaster response coordination, property-level mitigation, and the maintenance of flood defense systems. In a similar way, Grabs (2016) presents the Elbe floods of 2002 and 2013 as key triggers for the Elbe Flood Protection Action Plan and as a major moment in the broader acceptance of integrated flood risk management. These events matter not only because they caused severe damage, but because they exposed weaknesses in existing institutions and made flood governance a long-term political issue. Germany is thus a useful case because its current flood risk management system emerged through repeated extreme events, institutional learning, and ongoing policy adjustment.

[P00073 | 16005:16006 | NORMAL_TEXT]
⟦EMPTY PARAGRAPH⟧

[P00074 | 16006:17407 | NORMAL_TEXT]
German flood risk management operates across multiple governance levels and across different types of measures. At the European level, the Floods Directive created a common framework, while national and subnational actors remain responsible for implementation, planning, and financing in practice (Sairam et al., 2021). This multi-level structure means that flood risk management outcomes are shaped through coordination problems, regional variation, and institutional capacity, not only through technical necessity. The German debate has also moved beyond a narrow focus on dikes and flood walls. Grabs (2016) emphasizes that integrated flood management includes a mix of structural and non-structural measures, including warning systems, land-use regulation, reactivation of former floodplains, and broader adaptation strategies. Thieken et al. (2016) likewise stress the importance of spatial planning, preparedness, and public involvement. More recent work also shows that flood risk management increasingly includes private and semi-private dimensions, such as insurance, household mitigation, and municipality-level communication. Surminski and Thieken (2017) argue that insurance should be seen as part of a broader anticipatory flood risk management field, while Osberghaus (2015) shows that private mitigation remains necessary because public protection cannot eliminate flood risk entirely.

[P00075 | 17407:17408 | NORMAL_TEXT]
⟦EMPTY PARAGRAPH⟧

[P00076 | 17408:18527 | NORMAL_TEXT]
Despite this broader framing, German flood risk management still contains important tensions. On the one hand, public policy increasingly recognizes that absolute protection is neither technically nor economically feasible. Grabs (2016) explicitly argues that structural measures can mitigate flood hazards but cannot eliminate them, and that relying on engineering solutions alone may even increase residual risk and vulnerability in the event of failure. On the other hand, structural protection remains politically central because it is visible, state-led, and strongly associated with public safety. This creates a persistent tension between reactive and anticipatory approaches. Surminski and Thieken (2017) describe flood risk management in Germany and England as still largely reactive and event-driven, even though anticipatory approaches are widely discussed. Recent research on household adaptation in Germany supports this interpretation. Osberghaus (2015) and Dillenardt and Thieken (2025) both show that private adaptation, communication, and individual preparedness are important, but unevenly developed.

[P00077 | 18527:19343 | NORMAL_TEXT]
For this thesis, the German context is relevant in two ways. First, it provides an institutional setting in which flood risk management is formally broad enough to include hazard reduction, vulnerability reduction, planning, and preparedness. Second, structural protection infrastructure continues to play a major political and practical role. This duality matters for interpreting the distribution of exposure and modelled losses in the Elbe corridor, even though the thesis does not observe infrastructure directly. If German flood risk management is integrated in principle but uneven in practice, questions about the distribution of remaining burdens are unavoidable. Germany is therefore not merely the national background of the thesis, but the governance context that makes the research questions meaningful.

2.2 Area of Interest

[P00080 | 19365:20315 | NORMAL_TEXT]
The Elbe is one of the major river systems in Germany and has long been a central space of flood risk management, settlement development, and political intervention. In this thesis, the Elbe is not treated merely as a hydrological basin, but as a socio-spatial corridor in which flood dynamics, settlement patterns, and public protection strategies intersect. Repeated major flood events have made the Elbe one of the most visible river systems in German flood policy. Grabs (2016) highlights the Elbe floods of 2002 and 2013 as key triggers for the Elbe Flood Protection Action Plan, while Jüpner (2018) underlines that the Elbe flood disaster of 2013 again demonstrated both the destructive potential of extreme floods and the limits of technical protection. The Elbe is not just an empirically convenient case. It is a river corridor in which the relationships between hazard, protection, and social conditions can be observed especially clearly.

Historically, the Elbe has been shaped by a long interaction between settlement, river engineering, and flood experience. Jüpner (2018) shows that settlement along the Elbe originally concentrated on relatively elevated areas near the river, as in Magdeburg, where settlement historically remained more than 15 m above mean river level. Over time, however, population growth, land-use pressure, and hydraulic engineering transformed the river into an increasingly engineered system. According to Jüpner (2018), more than 500 km of the Elbe in Germany are now almost completely lined by dikes, and more than 83% of the natural floodplains along the river have been lost. For the present study, this illustrates that the Elbe is not a “natural” floodplain in any simple sense. It is a heavily modified river landscape in which exposure, protection, and residual risk are historically co-produced. Grabs (2016) makes a similar point by arguing that integrated flood management in the Elbe context must move beyond technical flood control alone and combine structural and non-structural strategies. The Elbe offers an especially useful setting for examining how flood exposure and flood protection are embedded in a broader socio-political landscape.

The Elbe is also a strong case because it has repeatedly been affected by severe flood events in recent decades. The major floods of 2002 and 2013 are especially relevant in this regard, but they are not the only events that matter. Barendrecht et al. (2018) discuss the Dresden case and show that the damage in 2013 was lower than in 2002 even though flood risk remained substantial, which they interpret as evidence that societal awareness and preparedness had changed after the earlier event. Jüpner (2018) likewise argues that the 2013 flood reopened debates on how flood risk should be managed, especially because two dike failures occurred and because areas assumed to be protected still suffered major damage. Earlier work by Lindenschmidt et al. (2006) further shows that large-basin risk assessment in the Elbe and Mulde context is methodologically demanding and was, at that time, still in its infancy. Together, these studies show that the Elbe is characterized not only by repeated flood exposure, but also by repeated institutional and social learning. The area of interest is not simply flood-prone; it is a landscape in which flood events have continuously reshaped risk perception, protection strategies, and governance debates.

The Elbe corridor is not only hydrologically heterogeneous, but also socially and economically uneven. This matters for the logic of the thesis because the river crosses regions that are embedded in broader East-West and urban-rural disparities within Germany. The 2024 report *Ost und West. A similar picture emerges from the broader regional disparity literature. Heider et al. (2023) argue that existing socio-economic disparities may intensify if effective preventive measures are lacking and stress the role of reliable indicators for identifying emerging patterns of inequality. Helbig (2023), in turn, shows that socio-spatial inequality in German cities is expressed through persistent concentrations of poverty, wealth, education, and social segregation. For the purposes of this thesis, these sources show that the Elbe corridor runs through municipalities with very different social and economic profiles. The study area cannot be understood only in terms of river morphology or floodplains. It must also be understood as a socially differentiated space in which vulnerability and adaptive capacity are unevenly distributed.

A flood-prone corridor that crosses municipalities with differing income structures, household profiles, demographic characteristics, and accessibility conditions creates a setting in which distributive questions become relevant. The issue is not only whether flood hazard exists, but whether modelled exposure and loss outcomes align with broader social inequalities. Odersky and Löffler (2024) show for the 2021 floods in Germany that exposure was regressive at the local level, with affected neighbourhoods displaying lower average incomes than nearby unaffected ones. Although their study concerns a different event and spatial setting, it reinforces the relevance of distributional analysis in Germany. The Elbe combines repeated flood experience, a long history of structural intervention, and a socially differentiated regional setting. This makes it well suited for investigating how flood exposure and modelled loss outcomes relate to socio-economic conditions.

2.3 Environmental and Flood Justice

[P00099 | 26609:27732 | NORMAL_TEXT]
Environmental justice provides the broader normative lens for this thesis because it shifts attention from floods as purely physical events to floods as socially differentiated forms of harm. In the environmental inequality literature, burdens are not assumed to be shared evenly across social groups and places; their distribution is linked to economic status, political power, and institutional capacity (Shao et al., 2022). This perspective is directly relevant for flood research, even though the field has often been dominated by hydrological and engineering approaches. Floods may be triggered by hydrometeorological processes, but who is exposed, who is protected, and who carries residual risk is not determined by nature alone. Liao et al. (2019), for example, argue that flood prevention is not a neutral technical exercise because interventions can redistribute floodwater rather than remove it. This theoretical insight provides the context for interpreting the empirical geography of exposure and modelled losses. 2.4 Socio-Hydrology and the Human Dimension of Risk

[P00114 | 35488:36597 | NORMAL_TEXT]

Flood losses are not determined by hazard alone, but by the long-term co-production of hazard, exposure, vulnerability, and adaptation. For this thesis, socio-hydrology provides the conceptual basis for linking flood exposure patterns to socio-economic structure. It suggests that the social geography of municipalities is not an external add-on to flood analysis, but part of the dynamics through which risk is produced and reproduced.

German flood-vulnerability research points in the same direction. Fekete (2009) notes that variable selection, thresholds, and aggregation involve assumptions, and that exact numerical weighting should be interpreted cautiously. This thesis responds to these concerns by treating the vulnerability index as an analytical tool rather than as a perfect measure of social reality. Its purpose is to reveal structured spatial patterns, not to assign immutable ranks to municipalities.

4.8 Vulnerability Index Construction and PCA Diagnostics

[P00268 | 85959:86964 | NORMAL_TEXT]
The final vulnerability index is constructed as a direction-coded and dimension-balanced composite rather than as a mechanically weighted PCA score. This choice follows from both the literature and the empirical structure of the corridor data. German river-flood vulnerability work has used factor-analytic approaches to identify dimensions of social vulnerability, but also emphasizes that aggregation and validation require substantive interpretation (Fekete, 2009). More broadly, social vulnerability indices are known to be sensitive to indicator choice, scale, weighting, and aggregation (Schmidtlein et al., 2008; Tate, 2013; Rufat et al., 2015). Rufat et al. (2019) further caution that vulnerability models should not be treated as self-validating simply because they produce an internally consistent score. PCA is used to inspect the structure and robustness of the selected indicators, while the main index is built to preserve the conceptual balance between the three vulnerability dimensions.

4.9 Multi-Return-Period Exposure Curves

[P00291 | 90786:91244 | NORMAL_TEXT]
The multi-return-period exposure table is also used to construct municipality-level exposure curves. Each municipality has a sequence of flood shares across `RP10`, `RP20`, `RP50`, `RP100`, `RP200`, and `RP500`. These curves describe how much of the final `RP500` exposure is already realized at more frequent return periods and how much is added only under rarer events. This preserves information that would be lost if the analysis relied only on `RP100`.

[P00292 | 91244:91245 | NORMAL_TEXT]

Several metrics are derived from these curves. `onset_rp_any` identifies the first return period with any positive flood share. `onset_rp_1pct` and `onset_rp_5pct` identify the first return period at which at least one or five percent of municipal area is flooded. `slope_logrp` measures the increase in flood share over the log-transformed return period scale. `normalized_auc` summarizes the area under the normalized exposure curve; higher values indicate that final exposure is realized earlier. `realized_by_rp100` measures the share of final RP500 exposure already present by RP100, while `late_growth_share` measures the share added after RP100. A delayed jump requires less than 50% realisation by RP100, at least 40% of final exposure being added after RP100, and the largest increase occurring between RP100 and RP200 or between RP200 and RP500. All remaining municipalities are classified as gradual increase. These thresholds are analytical classification rules rather than engineering protection standards.

5.8 Results Synthesis

[P00486 | 130091:130916 | NORMAL_TEXT]
The results answer the two research questions in different ways. For the first research question, the analysis does not find a simple positive relationship between socio-economic vulnerability and RP100 municipal area exposure. The RP100 exposure-vulnerability correlation is close to zero, and the quintile comparison is non-monotonic. The lowest-vulnerability quintile has the highest mean RP100 flood share, while the highest-vulnerability quintile still contains many strongly exposed municipalities but does not form a clear corridor-wide exposure maximum. The bivariate map confirms this: high-exposure and high-vulnerability municipalities exist, but high exposure also occurs among low- and medium-vulnerability municipalities. The exposure result is spatially heterogeneous rather than straightforwardly regressive.

[P00487 | 130916:130917 | NORMAL_TEXT]
⟦EMPTY PARAGRAPH⟧

[P00488 | 130917:131587 | NORMAL_TEXT]
The DLR extension makes this result more precise. Only `6.0%` of the classified RP100 flooded land is Artificial Land, which confirms that the total-area indicator largely reflects floodplain territory rather than built land alone. However, restricting the denominator to Artificial Land does not reveal a hidden positive vulnerability gradient. Artificial Land exposure remains weakly and slightly negatively correlated with the final vulnerability index, and the quintile pattern remains non-monotonic. The land-cover analysis therefore strengthens the first result as a robustness check, while stopping short of a claim about population, building, or asset exposure.

[P00489 | 131587:131588 | NORMAL_TEXT]
⟦EMPTY PARAGRAPH⟧

[P00490 | 131588:132292 | NORMAL_TEXT]
The multi-return-period analysis strengthens this interpretation. Most corridor municipalities are classified as early-exposure municipalities, and the mean exposure curves by vulnerability quintile remain broadly consistent across return periods. The RP100 benchmark does not appear to hide a fundamentally different vulnerability pattern at rarer events. It captures a corridor in which much of the eventual RP500 area exposure is already present by RP100 or earlier. The main exposure finding is thus not that vulnerable municipalities are uniformly more exposed, but that flood-area exposure is widespread, uneven, and only weakly associated with the final vulnerability index at municipality level.

[P00491 | 132292:132293 | NORMAL_TEXT]
⟦EMPTY PARAGRAPH⟧

[P00492 | 132293:132917 | NORMAL_TEXT]
The second research question produces a different pattern. Municipalities in the upper vulnerability groups more often have at least one modelled loss year than those in the lowest group. With no-event cases retained as p = 0, mean annual loss probability rises from 0.9% in Q1 to 4.8% in Q4 and 3.3% in Q5. Within the positive-loss subset, the median finite loss-return-period estimate declines from 156 years in Q1 to 28 years in Q5, with variation across the middle groups. 6.8 Limitations and Critical Reflection

[P00577 | 159314:160075 | NORMAL_TEXT]
Several limitations follow directly from the design of the thesis. The first is the municipality-level ecological design. Municipalities are meaningful administrative and analytical units, but they are internally heterogeneous. The analysis cannot identify household-level exposure, neighbourhood-scale segregation, or the social characteristics of the specific people and assets located inside the modelled flood footprint. It cannot make individual-level claims about who is personally exposed or harmed. This is a substantial limitation because flood inequalities often operate at fine spatial scales. The municipality-level results should be read as evidence about socially differentiated local contexts, not as direct evidence about individual households.

[P00578 | 160075:160076 | NORMAL_TEXT]
⟦EMPTY PARAGRAPH⟧

[P00579 | 160076:161369 | NORMAL_TEXT]
The second limitation concerns the exposure metrics. Total-area flood share is spatially consistent and transparent, but it is not depth-weighted, population-weighted, building-weighted, or asset-weighted. The DLR extension narrows this limitation by calculating the flooded share of Artificial Land, but the class is still a broad land-cover proxy. It does not distinguish housing from industry, transport infrastructure, or other artificial surfaces, and it does not show how many residents or assets are located in the flooded cells. The source represents land cover derived from Sentinel-2 imagery acquired between 2015 and 2017, so it also does not perfectly match all socio-economic and model reference years. Moreover, the extension is implemented for RP100 only. Neither exposure metric captures warning time, velocity, contamination, evacuation constraints, or other dimensions of flood severity. The EFAS/JRC hazard rasters used here are modelled large-scale river flood hazard maps, not official German hazard-zone maps (Baugh et al., 2024; Alfieri et al., 2014; Dottori et al., 2022). They are appropriate for consistent comparative exposure analysis, but they should not be interpreted as legal flood zones or as locally validated hydraulic studies for individual municipalities.

[P00580 | 161369:161370 | NORMAL_TEXT]
⟦EMPTY PARAGRAPH⟧

[P00581 | 161370:162102 | NORMAL_TEXT]
The third limitation concerns the vulnerability index. The index is based on INKAR indicators and on decisions about indicator selection, direction coding, imputation, dimension construction, and aggregation. These choices are documented and theoretically motivated, but they remain choices. Other defensible specifications could produce somewhat different spatial patterns. One corridor municipality, Berga-Wünschendorf, lacks a current INKAR match and has no vulnerability-index value. More broadly, the index captures socio-economic vulnerability at the municipal level; it does not measure individual coping capacity, social networks, health status, household savings, insurance coverage, or previous flood experience directly.

7.4 Outlook for Research and Practice

[P00637 | 179117:179941 | NORMAL_TEXT]
Several extensions follow directly from the limits of this thesis. The DLR Artificial Land indicator already moves beyond a total municipal-area denominator, but it remains a broad land-cover proxy. This would distinguish residential areas from industrial and transport surfaces and separate municipalities with large flooded territories from municipalities where people, buildings, or assets are concentrated inside the modelled flood footprint. Such extensions would be especially useful for high-exposure and high-vulnerability municipalities and for municipalities with high EFAS/JRC exposure but no simulated loss events in the provider portfolio.
