## Environmental & Flood Justice

Environmental and flood justice form the normative backbone of this thesis because they make it possible to understand floods not only as physical events, but also as socially differentiated risks. In the broader environmental justice literature, the central concern is that environmental harms and environmental benefits are not distributed equally across society. These inequalities do not emerge only from “nature”, but also from planning decisions, institutional priorities, and the unequal political power of different social groups. In the context of flooding, this perspective is highly relevant because flood management measures can create protection for some areas while increasing or maintaining risks for others. Liao et al. (2019) argue explicitly that flood prevention, although often framed as a technical undertaking, can generate environmental injustice because it redistributes floodwater and therefore redistributes risk. A broader review by Liu et al. (2021) similarly stresses that environmental inequality refers to the uneven exposure of different groups and places to environmental burdens. For the present thesis, these arguments are important because they justify treating flood exposure and flood protection as questions of social distribution rather than as purely hydrological facts.

Within this broader field, flood justice can be understood as a more specific concern with how flood-related burdens, benefits, and decision processes are distributed. The literature increasingly emphasizes that flood risk management cannot be reduced to a neutral engineering exercise. De Goër de Herve (2022) shows that justice considerations in flood risk management are closely connected to questions of fairness, equity, and sustainability. Likewise, Thaler and Hartmann (2016) argue that flood protection always raises distributive dilemmas because some actors benefit from protective measures while others may lose, whether through lower protection, redirected water, or unequal allocation of public resources. Natural and imposed inequalities are therefore intertwined. Johnson et al. (2007) describe flooding as “not fair per se” because natural exposure is already spatially uneven, but they also show that public policy can amplify, mitigate, or legitimize these inequalities. This is a crucial point for the thesis: if flood risk is already spatially uneven, then the task of flood risk management is not only to reduce hazard, but also to decide how protection, compensation, and acceptable risk are distributed.

A key strength of the flood justice literature is that it distinguishes different dimensions of justice instead of treating fairness as a single idea. De Goër de Herve (2022) differentiates especially between distributive and procedural justice, and also links justice in flood risk management to wider social, spatial, ecological, and temporal concerns. Liao et al. (2019) additionally show that recognition matters, because marginalized communities are often not fully acknowledged as legitimate stakeholders when flood prevention plans are designed. Justice in flood governance therefore concerns at least three questions: who bears the burden of flood exposure, who benefits from protective interventions, and who is included in the decisions that shape these outcomes. For this thesis, however, the main emphasis lies on distributive justice. The core empirical interest is not primarily whether procedural participation was fair, but whether socially vulnerable municipalities are more exposed to flood hazards and whether the distribution of protection-related benefits appears socially uneven. Procedural and recognition justice remain important background dimensions, but they are not the main empirical focus of the analysis.

This distributive perspective becomes especially relevant once flood justice is linked to social vulnerability and socio-economic inequality. The literature on flood disadvantage makes clear that flood risk is rarely isolated from broader patterns of deprivation. Sayers et al. (2018) argue that flood disadvantage emerges through the compounding interaction between flooding and other forms of social disadvantage across different policy domains and spatial scales. Similar concerns appear in work on the social consequences of flood management itself. Ziervogel et al. (2021) show that weak governance and structural inequalities can make flood risk management reinforce poverty and social inequality rather than reduce it. More directly for Europe and Germany, Poussard et al. (2021) show that flood exposure is scale-sensitive and socially differentiated, while Odersky and Löffler (2024) demonstrate that the 2021 floods in Germany had a clearly regressive exposure pattern at the local level. Tovar Reaños (2021) further finds that welfare losses from floods disproportionately harm low-income households and can increase inequality. These studies matter for the present thesis because they suggest that the social significance of flooding cannot be captured by hazard alone. Exposure must be interpreted together with the socio-economic capacity to anticipate, cope with, and recover from flood impacts.

For this reason, the thesis treats environmental and flood justice as an analytical framework for comparing the spatial distribution of flood-related outcomes with the spatial distribution of socio-economic vulnerability. The central question is not simply where floods occur, but whether municipalities with higher social vulnerability bear a disproportionate share of flood exposure and potentially a weaker share of protection-related benefits. This does not mean that every observed inequality automatically proves injustice in a normative or legal sense. As Thaler and Hartmann (2016) and de Goër de Herve (2022) both show, flood risk management involves competing notions of fairness, different territorial priorities, and difficult trade-offs between efficiency, protection, and social equity. Nevertheless, the literature is clear that there are strong reasons to examine flood governance through a justice lens whenever risks, resources, and protection are distributed unequally. In this thesis, environmental and flood justice therefore provide the conceptual basis for asking how flood hazard, exposure, vulnerability, and protection intersect across municipalities in the Elbe context, and whether these intersections point to socially uneven burdens or benefits.
