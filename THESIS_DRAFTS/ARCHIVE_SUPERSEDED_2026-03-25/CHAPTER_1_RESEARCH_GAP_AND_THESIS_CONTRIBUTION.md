## Research Gap & Thesis Contribution

The literature on floods, vulnerability, and flood risk management is already substantial, and the present thesis does not start from an empty field. A large body of work has examined hydrological hazard, damage estimation, governance reform, and the social dimensions of vulnerability. In parallel, a growing international literature has shown that flood exposure is often socially uneven rather than randomly distributed. Qiang (2019) demonstrates this for the United States at national scale, while Poussard et al. (2021) show that environmental inequalities in flood exposure are strongly shaped by the spatial scale at which they are measured. More recent work for Latin America reaches a similar conclusion and stresses that substantial social disparities in flood exposure can also be identified at the local level (Kephart et al., 2025). German research has also moved in this direction. Tovar Reaños (2021) links flood damages to inequality in welfare terms, and Odersky and Löffler (2024) provide evidence that flood exposure in Germany was regressive at the local scale during the 2021 event. Taken together, these studies make clear that the relationship between flooding and social inequality is no longer a marginal topic. It has become an increasingly visible research field in its own right.

At the same time, an important gap remains. Existing studies often focus on one dimension in relative isolation: physical hazard and damage, social vulnerability measurement, or governance and protection strategies. The challenge is that these strands are not always brought together within one coherent distributive framework. This is especially relevant because flood inequality is, as Poussin et al. (2021) argue, a matter of scale. Patterns that appear weak or invisible at broader regional levels can become much clearer when the analysis moves to smaller spatial units. Older German work already pointed to this problem. Fekete (2010) argued that an overview of social vulnerability to river floods in Germany was missing and emphasized that only few studies had addressed the non-structural and socially differentiated dimensions of flood risk in a systematic way. Rufat et al. (2015) add a broader methodological caution by showing that there remains a large gap between the contextual complexity of social vulnerability and the generalized indicator practices often used to measure it. Tate (2019) makes a similar point from another angle by arguing that many social vulnerability models remain primarily descriptive. For the purposes of this thesis, the implication is not that previous work is inadequate. Rather, it is that there is still limited municipality-level evidence that directly links flood exposure patterns with socio-economic vulnerability in a justice-oriented way for the Elbe corridor.

This is where the present thesis is positioned. Its contribution lies in combining a municipality-based exposure analysis with a socio-economic vulnerability perspective in one analytically coherent framework. Empirically, the thesis focuses on the Elbe flood corridor and examines how flood exposure is distributed across municipalities with different social characteristics. In doing so, it responds directly to the scale problem identified in the literature and to the need for more spatially explicit evidence on distributive patterns of flood risk. At the same time, it builds on the German vulnerability literature without simply reproducing it. The thesis does not only map socio-economic vulnerability, nor does it restrict itself to hazard or damage alone. Instead, it links flood exposure to a multidimensional vulnerability index and keeps the broader question of protection conceptually central to the analysis of unequal flood-related outcomes. The contribution is therefore both empirical and conceptual: it adds new municipality-level evidence for the Elbe corridor and situates that evidence within the broader debate on flood justice, socio-economic vulnerability, and spatial inequality.
