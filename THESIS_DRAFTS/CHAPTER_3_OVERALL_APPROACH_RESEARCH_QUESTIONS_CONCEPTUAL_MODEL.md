## Overall Approach

Building on the theoretical and conceptual discussion of Chapter 2, this thesis adopts a spatially explicit municipality-level design for the analysis of flood exposure and socio-economic vulnerability in the Elbe context. The study is conceived as a distributive comparison rather than as a purely hydrological assessment or a purely institutional analysis. Its central aim is to identify whether municipalities with different socio-economic profiles also occupy systematically different positions in the geography of flood exposure and, later, in the geography of protection-related differentiation. This general orientation follows the broader literature on environmental inequalities in flood exposure, which stresses that the relationship between social differentiation and flood hazard is meaningful only when it is examined at an appropriate spatial scale and within a clearly defined study setting (Poussard et al., 2021).

The thesis remains framed in terms of the Elbe river basin, because the basin provides the broader hydrological, historical, and governance context in which flood management and settlement development have evolved. Empirically, however, the analysis focuses on the flood-relevant Elbe corridor. This distinction is important. A basin-wide inclusion of municipalities would also capture places that are hydrologically connected to the Elbe system but not substantively relevant for a comparison of flood exposure. De Moel et al. (2015) emphasize that flood-risk assessments differ substantially across spatial scales and study extents, while Sairam et al. (2021) show that spatial consistency matters strongly for the interpretation of flood patterns. The revised design therefore defines the study population through the extreme flood-relevant corridor rather than through the full basin alone. In practical terms, municipalities are retained if they intersect the `RP500` flood extent. This sharpens the analytical focus without abandoning the broader basin framing of the thesis.

Within this corridor-based sample, the thesis follows a staged analytical strategy. The first step is to establish a robust exposure-vulnerability framework at municipality level. Flood exposure is derived consistently across several return periods, while socio-economic vulnerability is represented through a municipality-level indicator framework based on INKAR. This provides the empirical basis for the first research question. The second step concerns protection. Protection remains conceptually central to the thesis, but its empirical treatment requires greater caution than a simple binary protected / unprotected distinction would allow. Research on residual risk and socio-hydrological feedbacks has shown repeatedly that protection can reduce frequent damage while still leaving substantial risk in place under more extreme conditions (Fu et al., 2023; Serra-Llobet et al., 2022; Tobin, 1995). For this reason, the thesis first develops a strong exposure-vulnerability design and then extends the analysis toward protection-related interpretation in a more careful and transparent way.

## Research Questions

The thesis is guided by two fixed research questions:

1. How is flood hazard and exposure distributed across socio-economic groups in the Elbe River basin?
2. What role does flood protection infrastructure play in shaping this distribution?

Under the revised design, these questions remain unchanged, but they are addressed through a more sharply defined empirical strategy. The first question is approached directly through the municipality-level comparison of flood exposure and socio-economic vulnerability within the flood-relevant Elbe corridor. In this context, “socio-economic groups” are not observed as individuals or neighborhoods, but approximated through municipality-level social profiles. This means that the thesis does not claim to identify intra-municipal sorting or household-level inequality. Rather, it examines whether municipalities with different socio-economic characteristics systematically occupy different positions in the geography of flood exposure. This remains fully consistent with the question as stated, while also matching the scale at which the available data can be integrated coherently. As de Moel et al. (2015) argue, methodology and interpretation vary substantially across spatial scales. Odersky and Löffler (2024) make a similar point for Germany by showing that differential flood exposure becomes especially visible when it is examined at a local level and within a more comparable regional context.

The second question remains equally central, but it requires a more careful interpretation. In earlier stages of the project, protection was treated more directly through infrastructure-related datasets. The revised design is more cautious. It retains protection as an essential conceptual part of the thesis, because distributive flood justice cannot be evaluated only through hazard and exposure. At the same time, it acknowledges that protection is difficult to observe in a simple and spatially consistent way. The thesis therefore does not assume from the outset that protection can be represented as a straightforward municipality-level binary attribute. Instead, the role of protection is addressed in a staged manner: first by building the exposure-vulnerability comparison cleanly, and then by examining how protection-related differentiation may shape this relationship. This approach is analytically stronger than forcing a weak operationalization too early. It also corresponds to the broader flood-justice literature. De Goër de Herve (2022) emphasizes that justice claims in flood risk management require careful interpretation, while Johnson et al. (2007) show that uneven flood outcomes do not automatically distinguish between “natural” and socially imposed injustices.

Taken together, the two research questions define the thesis as a distributive inquiry rather than as a narrowly causal or engineering-based one. The empirical goal is to identify whether socially differentiated municipalities also experience systematically differentiated flood-related outcomes. If such a pattern exists, it becomes analytically meaningful in the language of justice. If it does not, this is equally informative, because it suggests that flood exposure and protection-related differentiation are not strongly aligned with the socio-economic geography of the corridor. In either case, the questions are designed to evaluate spatial patterning rather than to prove intent, moral fault, or a single deterministic mechanism. This point is important, because, as Rufat et al. (2019) argue, social vulnerability models are highly useful for structured comparison, but their explanatory reach should not be overstated.

## Conceptual Model

Chapter 2 already established the conceptual triad of exposure, vulnerability, and protection. The purpose of the present section is therefore not to redefine these concepts, but to translate them into the analytical model of the study. At the center of this model is the municipality. For each municipality in the flood-relevant corridor, the thesis links flood exposure information to a socio-economic vulnerability profile and later interprets this relationship in light of protection-related differentiation. In this sense, the analytical model is a spatial comparison of municipal exposure and municipal vulnerability within one coherent study population.

On the exposure side, the thesis works with a multi-return-period flood dataset at municipality level, including `RP10`, `RP20`, `RP50`, `RP100`, `RP200`, and `RP500`. This allows the analysis to move beyond one isolated hazard snapshot and to retain information about how exposure changes across return periods. For the main comparative analysis, `RP100` functions as the current benchmark, because it is both policy-relevant and analytically interpretable. On the vulnerability side, the thesis uses a municipality-level socio-economic index derived from INKAR. The broader literature suggests that vulnerability should be approached as a multidimensional phenomenon rather than reduced to one social marker such as income alone. Rufat et al. (2015) identify demographic characteristics, socio-economic status, and health among the leading drivers of flood vulnerability, while Fekete (2009, 2010) demonstrates for Germany that social vulnerability to river floods can be operationalized meaningfully at subnational scale. The analytical model of this thesis therefore links a multidimensional vulnerability profile to a consistent municipal exposure dataset.

Protection enters this analytical model in a more cautious way. The thesis does not treat protection as a simple binary status assigned to municipalities. Instead, it is understood as a factor that shapes how physical hazard becomes realized exposure and remaining risk. This matters because, as Tobin (1995) and later Serra-Llobet et al. (2022) and Fu et al. (2023) show, structural protection may reduce frequent losses while still leaving considerable residual risk under more extreme conditions. For the purposes of the analytical model, this means that the main empirical comparison first concerns exposure and vulnerability, while protection-related differentiation is considered as an additional interpretive dimension rather than as a fully resolved input at the outset. The resulting model is therefore simple in structure, but careful in interpretation: it compares the geography of municipal flood exposure with the geography of municipal social vulnerability and then asks whether this relationship remains stable once protection-related factors are brought into the analysis.
