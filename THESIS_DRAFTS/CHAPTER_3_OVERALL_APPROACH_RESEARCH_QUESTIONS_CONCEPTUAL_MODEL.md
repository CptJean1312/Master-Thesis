## Overall Approach

Building on the theoretical and conceptual discussion of Chapter 2, this thesis adopts a spatially explicit municipality-level design for the analysis of flood exposure and socio-economic vulnerability in the Elbe context. The study is conceived as a distributive comparison rather than as a purely hydrological assessment or a purely institutional analysis. Its central aim is to identify whether municipalities with different socio-economic profiles also occupy systematically different positions in the geography of flood exposure and, later, in the geography of protection-related differentiation. This general orientation follows the broader literature on environmental inequalities in flood exposure, which stresses that the relationship between social differentiation and flood hazard is meaningful only when it is examined at an appropriate spatial scale and within a clearly defined study setting (Poussard et al., 2021).

The thesis remains framed in terms of the Elbe river basin, because the basin provides the broader hydrological, historical, and governance context in which flood management and settlement development have evolved. Empirically, however, the analysis focuses on the flood-relevant Elbe corridor. This distinction is important. A basin-wide inclusion of municipalities would also capture places that are hydrologically connected to the Elbe system but not substantively relevant for a comparison of flood exposure. De Moel et al. (2015) emphasize that flood-risk assessments differ substantially across spatial scales and study extents, while Sairam et al. (2021) show that spatial consistency matters strongly for the interpretation of flood patterns. The revised design therefore defines the study population through the extreme flood-relevant corridor rather than through the full basin alone. In practical terms, municipalities are retained if they intersect the `RP500` flood extent. This sharpens the analytical focus without abandoning the broader basin framing of the thesis.

Within this corridor-based sample, the thesis follows a staged analytical strategy. The first step is to establish a robust exposure-vulnerability framework at municipality level. Flood exposure is derived consistently across several return periods, while socio-economic vulnerability is represented through a municipality-level indicator framework based on INKAR. This provides the empirical basis for the first research question. The second step concerns protection. Protection remains conceptually central to the thesis, but its empirical treatment requires greater caution than a simple binary protected / unprotected distinction would allow. Research on residual risk and socio-hydrological feedbacks has shown repeatedly that protection can reduce frequent damage while still leaving substantial risk in place under more extreme conditions (Fu et al., 2023; Serra-Llobet et al., 2022; Tobin, 1995). For this reason, the thesis first develops a strong exposure-vulnerability design and then extends the analysis toward protection-related interpretation in a more careful and transparent way.

## Research Questions

The thesis is guided by two fixed research questions:

1. How is flood hazard and exposure distributed across socio-economic groups in the Elbe River basin?
2. What role does flood protection infrastructure play in shaping this distribution?

Under the revised design, these questions remain unchanged, but they are addressed through a more sharply defined empirical strategy. The first question is approached directly through the municipality-level comparison of flood exposure and socio-economic vulnerability within the flood-relevant Elbe corridor. In this context, “socio-economic groups” are not observed as individuals or neighborhoods, but approximated through municipality-level social profiles. This means that the thesis does not claim to identify intra-municipal sorting or household-level inequality. Rather, it examines whether municipalities with different socio-economic characteristics systematically occupy different positions in the geography of flood exposure. This remains fully consistent with the question as stated, while also matching the scale at which the available data can be integrated coherently. As de Moel et al. (2015) argue, methodology and interpretation vary substantially across spatial scales. Odersky and Löffler (2024) make a similar point for Germany by showing that differential flood exposure becomes especially visible when it is examined at a local level and within a more comparable regional context.

The second question remains equally central, but it requires a more careful interpretation. In earlier stages of the project, protection was treated more directly through infrastructure-related datasets. The revised design is more cautious. It retains protection as an essential conceptual part of the thesis, because distributive flood justice cannot be evaluated only through hazard and exposure. At the same time, it acknowledges that protection is difficult to observe in a simple and spatially consistent way. The thesis therefore does not assume from the outset that protection can be represented as a straightforward municipality-level binary attribute. Instead, the role of protection is addressed in a staged manner: first by building the exposure-vulnerability comparison cleanly, and then by examining how protection-related differentiation may shape this relationship. This approach is analytically stronger than forcing a weak operationalization too early. It also corresponds to the broader flood-justice literature. De Goër de Herve (2022) emphasizes that justice claims in flood risk management require careful interpretation, while Johnson et al. (2007) show that uneven flood outcomes do not automatically distinguish between “natural” and socially imposed injustices.

Taken together, the two research questions define the thesis as a distributive inquiry rather than as a narrowly causal or engineering-based one. The empirical goal is to identify whether socially differentiated municipalities also experience systematically differentiated flood-related outcomes. If such a pattern exists, it becomes analytically meaningful in the language of justice. If it does not, this is equally informative, because it suggests that flood exposure and protection-related differentiation are not strongly aligned with the socio-economic geography of the corridor. In either case, the questions are designed to evaluate spatial patterning rather than to prove intent, moral fault, or a single deterministic mechanism. This point is important, because, as Rufat et al. (2019) argue, social vulnerability models are highly useful for structured comparison, but their explanatory reach should not be overstated.

## Conceptual Model

Chapter 2 already established the conceptual triad of exposure, vulnerability, and protection. The purpose of the present section is therefore not to redefine these concepts, but to translate them into the analytical model of the study. At the center of this model is the municipality. For each municipality in the flood-relevant corridor, the thesis links flood exposure information to a socio-economic vulnerability profile and later interprets this relationship in light of protection-related differentiation. In this sense, the analytical model is a spatial comparison of municipal exposure and municipal vulnerability within one coherent study population.

On the exposure side, the thesis works with a multi-return-period flood dataset at municipality level, including `RP10`, `RP20`, `RP50`, `RP100`, `RP200`, and `RP500`. This allows the analysis to move beyond one isolated hazard snapshot and to retain information about how exposure changes across return periods. For the main comparative analysis, `RP100` functions as the current benchmark, because it is both policy-relevant and analytically interpretable. On the vulnerability side, the thesis uses a municipality-level socio-economic index derived from INKAR. The broader literature suggests that vulnerability should be approached as a multidimensional phenomenon rather than reduced to one social marker such as income alone. Rufat et al. (2015) identify demographic characteristics, socio-economic status, and health among the leading drivers of flood vulnerability, while Fekete (2009, 2010) demonstrates for Germany that social vulnerability to river floods can be operationalized meaningfully at subnational scale. The analytical model of this thesis therefore links a multidimensional vulnerability profile to a consistent municipal exposure dataset.

Protection enters this analytical model in a more cautious way. The thesis does not treat protection as a simple binary status assigned to municipalities. Instead, it is understood as a factor that shapes how physical hazard becomes realized exposure and remaining risk. This matters because, as Tobin (1995) and later Serra-Llobet et al. (2022) and Fu et al. (2023) show, structural protection may reduce frequent losses while still leaving considerable residual risk under more extreme conditions. For the purposes of the analytical model, this means that the main empirical comparison first concerns exposure and vulnerability, while protection-related differentiation is considered as an additional interpretive dimension rather than as a fully resolved input at the outset. The resulting model is therefore simple in structure, but careful in interpretation: it compares the geography of municipal flood exposure with the geography of municipal social vulnerability and then asks whether this relationship remains stable once protection-related factors are brought into the analysis.

## Spatial Unit of Analysis

The municipality forms the central unit of analysis in this thesis because it provides the most coherent scale at which the available flood and socio-economic data can be combined while still retaining meaningful spatial variation. This choice is not only practical, but also analytical. Flood-justice research has shown repeatedly that the relationship between social inequality and flood exposure is sensitive to spatial scale. De Moel et al. (2015) emphasize that flood-risk assessments differ markedly across scales and that both methodology and interpretation must be adjusted accordingly. Poussard et al. (2021) make the same point more directly in the context of environmental inequalities, showing that the apparent relationship between socio-economic status and flood exposure can change when the scale of analysis shifts from broader regions to smaller local units. For this reason, the municipality is used here as a middle scale that is local enough to reveal distributive spatial differences, but still broad enough to allow a consistent integration of flood rasters, administrative boundaries, and socio-economic indicators.

This scale also fits the German context particularly well. Municipalities are not only statistical units, but important governance units within flood risk management, spatial planning, and public service provision. In that sense, they are meaningful territorial entities through which spatial inequality can be described and later interpreted. German flood-risk research likewise often works with subnational or aggregated administrative units when linking physical hazard to social or economic information. Sairam et al. (2021), for example, stress the importance of spatial consistency in large-scale flood risk assessment for Germany, while Fekete (2009, 2010) demonstrates that social vulnerability to river floods can be operationalized meaningfully at subnational scale. The municipality therefore offers a defensible compromise between fine-scale sensitivity and analytical feasibility. It is not as detailed as a neighborhood or building-level study, but it is substantially more informative than broad county, regional, or basin-wide averages.

The empirical study population is defined through municipalities that intersect the `RP500` flood extent. This step is important because it aligns the unit of analysis with the flood-relevant study corridor instead of with the entire Elbe basin. The basin remains the broader hydrological and governance frame of the thesis, but not every municipality in the basin is equally relevant for a justice-oriented comparison of flood exposure. By restricting the sample to municipalities that are actually part of the extreme flood corridor, the analysis becomes more coherent and more comparable across cases. At the same time, the municipality-level perspective makes it possible to connect the exposure analysis with the socio-economic structure of local territories. This does not mean that municipalities are internally homogeneous, nor that socio-economic groups are perfectly represented by municipal averages. It means, rather, that the thesis compares socially differentiated local contexts within one flood-relevant corridor and asks whether these contexts are associated with systematically different positions in the geography of flood exposure.

## Equity Assessment Logic

The equity assessment of this thesis is primarily distributive. Its core question is whether flood-related burdens and, later, protection-related advantages are patterned systematically across municipalities with different socio-economic profiles. This logic follows the flood-justice literature, which argues that fairness in flood risk management cannot be reduced to technical efficiency alone. Thaler and Hartmann (2016) show that flood risk management always involves decisions about who or what should be protected, while de Goër de Herve (2022) emphasizes that justice in this field includes both distributive and procedural dimensions. Building on these debates, the thesis focuses empirically on the distributive side. It asks whether municipalities that are more socially vulnerable also tend to face greater flood exposure or weaker apparent protection-related benefits. In that sense, equity is not treated as a moral slogan, but as an analytical question about the spatial alignment of social disadvantage and flood-related outcomes.

Operationally, this means comparing the geography of municipal vulnerability with the geography of municipal flood exposure. The first analytical step is therefore straightforward: a municipality-level socio-economic vulnerability profile is related to municipal flood-area shares, with `RP100` serving as the main benchmark and the broader multi-return-period structure retained for additional interpretation. This design does not assume that equal exposure across space is the only fair outcome, nor does it assume that every spatial difference is automatically unjust. As Johnson et al. (2007) argue, flooding is not fair in any simple natural sense, because hazard is inherently uneven across space. The justice-relevant issue begins when this physical unevenness overlaps systematically with social disadvantage or when public and private protection-related benefits are distributed in ways that reinforce rather than reduce existing inequalities. The empirical task of the thesis is therefore to identify whether such overlaps occur in the Elbe corridor and how strong they appear to be at municipality level.

This distributive logic also requires a cautious interpretation of what the analysis can show. The thesis does not identify the intentions of decision-makers, and it does not claim to isolate one single causal mechanism through which inequality and exposure become linked. Instead, it evaluates a justice-relevant spatial pattern. This is consistent with recent German and international work showing that differential flood exposure can be observed empirically at relatively local scales, even if the mechanisms behind it remain multiple and historically layered. Odersky and Löffler (2024) show that the 2021 floods in Germany displayed a regressive exposure pattern at the local level, while Tovar Reaños (2021) demonstrates that flood impacts and flood policy can interact with welfare and inequality in ways that disproportionately affect low-income households. For the present thesis, the implication is clear: if more vulnerable municipalities in the Elbe corridor are systematically more exposed, this is analytically meaningful as a distributive justice pattern, even if it does not by itself prove intentional discrimination or a single policy failure.

## Scope, Assumptions & Limits

The design of this thesis is deliberately bounded, and these boundaries are important for interpreting its results correctly. First, the study is not a full-basin census of all municipalities that are hydrologically linked to the Elbe. It is a corridor-based analysis of municipalities intersecting the `RP500` flood extent. This improves flood relevance and comparability, but it also means that the results apply to the flood-relevant Elbe corridor rather than to the whole basin in an undifferentiated sense. Second, the municipality is an administrative and analytical unit, not a social group in itself. Municipal averages can reveal meaningful spatial differences, but they cannot capture within-municipal sorting, neighborhood-level heterogeneity, or household-level experiences. This scale issue is well known in both flood-risk and environmental-inequality research. De Moel et al. (2015) and Poussard et al. (2021) both show that findings can vary across scales, while Odersky and Löffler (2024) explicitly note the value of finer local evidence for identifying differential exposure. The present study therefore makes a disciplined ecological claim: it compares socially differentiated municipalities, not individual households.

The exposure side of the analysis is also subject to clear scope conditions. The municipal flood indicators used here are area-based measures derived from modeled flood extents across several return periods. Their current primary role is to quantify how much of each municipality lies within the modeled flood footprint, especially for `RP100`, which functions as the main benchmark. This is a transparent and spatially consistent measure, but it is not identical to population exposure, building exposure, or monetary damage. A municipality may contain substantial flooded area while its settlement, population, or asset concentration is distributed unevenly within that area. More generally, large-scale flood assessments are always shaped by simplifications in hazard modeling, exposure representation, and the treatment of return periods. Sairam et al. (2021) underline the importance of spatial consistency in German large-scale risk assessment, while earlier European work also highlights considerable uncertainty in large-return-period estimates and in the translation from flood depth and extent into impact metrics (Dottori et al., 2016). These issues do not invalidate area-based exposure analysis, but they define what the indicator can and cannot represent.

The vulnerability side requires similar caution. Social vulnerability indices are useful because they reduce complex, multidimensional social conditions to a structured and comparable spatial measure. At the same time, the literature is clear that such indices are sensitive to methodological choices. Schmidtlein et al. (2008) and Tate (2013) show that results can change substantially depending on indicator selection, normalization, weighting, aggregation, and scale. Rufat et al. (2019) likewise argue that most social vulnerability models are primarily descriptive and should not be treated as self-validating explanatory instruments. German flood-vulnerability research points in the same direction. Fekete (2009) explicitly notes that variable selection, thresholds, and aggregation involve assumptions, and that exact numerical weighting should be interpreted cautiously. This thesis responds to these concerns by treating the vulnerability index as an analytical tool rather than as a perfect measure of social reality. Its purpose is to reveal structured spatial patterns, not to assign immutable ranks to municipalities.

Finally, the treatment of protection in this thesis remains intentionally cautious. Protection is conceptually central, because the distribution of flood-related burdens cannot be understood fully without considering how hazard is modified, delayed, or reduced by protective arrangements. However, protection is not yet introduced here as a fully resolved municipality-level variable with a simple binary meaning. The design therefore proceeds in stages. It first establishes a robust exposure-vulnerability comparison and only then extends toward protection-related interpretation. This should be understood as a methodological decision, not as a conceptual gap. Research on residual risk, levee effects, and socio-hydrological feedbacks shows clearly that protection is socially consequential but difficult to reduce to a single, spatially unambiguous indicator (Serra-Llobet et al., 2022; Fu et al., 2023). For that reason, the present design prefers transparency over premature precision. The study does not claim more than its data can support, but it is precisely this restraint that makes the analytical framework more defensible.
