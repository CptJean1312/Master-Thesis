## Overall Approach

This thesis is best understood as a spatially explicit study of flood exposure, socio-economic vulnerability, and protection-related differentiation at municipality level in the Elbe context. It is not a purely hydrological flood-risk assessment, because the central interest is not only the physical extent of flooding. At the same time, it is not a purely institutional study of flood governance. Rather, it combines both perspectives through a distributive justice lens. As Poussard et al. (2021) show, the spatial distribution of flood exposure becomes analytically meaningful once it is examined together with socio-economic differentiation and the scale at which both are compared. In a similar way, Thaler and Hartmann (2016) argue that flood risk management inevitably raises questions about who is protected and how risks and benefits are distributed. The basic approach of this thesis therefore is to compare flood-related spatial patterns with the socio-economic structure of municipalities and to ask whether this relationship points to systematically uneven burdens or advantages.

The study remains conceptually anchored in the Elbe river basin, because the basin represents the broader hydrological, historical, and governance setting within which flood management, settlement development, and public intervention have evolved. Empirically, however, the analysis is narrowed to the flood-relevant Elbe corridor. This distinction is important. A basin-wide inclusion of municipalities would also capture places that are hydrologically connected to the Elbe system but not meaningfully relevant for a comparison of flood exposure. De Moel et al. (2015) emphasize that flood-risk assessments differ substantially across spatial scales and study extents, while Sairam et al. (2021) show that spatial consistency matters strongly for the interpretation of flood patterns. The revised design therefore defines the study population through the extreme flood-relevant corridor rather than through the full basin alone. In practical terms, municipalities are retained if they intersect the `RP500` flood extent. This sharpens the analytical focus without abandoning the broader basin framing of the thesis.

Within this corridor-based sample, the thesis follows a staged analytical strategy. The first step is to establish a robust exposure-vulnerability framework at municipality level. Flood exposure is derived consistently across several return periods, while socio-economic vulnerability is represented through a multidimensional indicator framework based on INKAR. This creates the empirical basis for the core distributive question of the thesis, namely whether more socially vulnerable municipalities also tend to face greater flood exposure. The second step concerns protection. Protection remains conceptually central, because distributive flood justice cannot be evaluated through hazard and exposure alone. However, the thesis does not treat protection as a simple binary condition. As Tobin (1995) argued in his critique of the “levee love affair,” structural protection can reduce frequent losses while also reshaping settlement and risk perception. More recent work by Serra-Llobet et al. (2022) and Fu et al. (2023) likewise stresses that protection remains partial and that residual risk persists even in apparently protected settings. For this reason, the thesis first develops a strong exposure-vulnerability design and then extends the analysis toward protection-related interpretation in a more cautious way.

This overall approach follows directly from the theoretical logic developed in Chapter 2. If flood exposure is spatially uneven, if socio-economic vulnerability is spatially uneven, and if protection influences how physical hazard becomes actual exposure and remaining risk, then the relevant empirical task is to compare these geographies within one coherent framework. The municipality is used as the key spatial unit because it allows the integration of flood exposure and socio-economic information within one comparable administrative scale, while still retaining enough spatial variation for a meaningful distributive analysis. At the same time, this approach remains methodologically cautious. Rufat et al. (2015) emphasize that vulnerability indicators must reflect the context in which vulnerability occurs, and Rufat et al. (2019) remind us that social vulnerability models are often descriptive rather than directly explanatory. The design of this thesis therefore is explicitly comparative and justice-oriented, but also careful about the limits of what can be inferred from municipality-level patterns alone.

## Research Questions

The thesis is guided by two fixed research questions:

1. How is flood hazard and exposure distributed across socio-economic groups in the Elbe River basin?
2. What role does flood protection infrastructure play in shaping this distribution?

Under the revised design, these questions remain unchanged, but they are addressed through a more sharply defined empirical strategy. The first question is approached directly through the municipality-level comparison of flood exposure and socio-economic vulnerability within the flood-relevant Elbe corridor. In this context, “socio-economic groups” are not observed as individuals or neighborhoods, but approximated through municipality-level social profiles. This means that the thesis does not claim to identify intra-municipal sorting or household-level inequality. Rather, it examines whether municipalities with different socio-economic characteristics systematically occupy different positions in the geography of flood exposure. This remains fully consistent with the question as stated, while also matching the scale at which the available data can be integrated coherently. As de Moel et al. (2015) argue, methodology and interpretation vary substantially across spatial scales. Odersky and Löffler (2024) make a similar point for Germany by showing that differential flood exposure becomes especially visible when it is examined at a local level and within a more comparable regional context.

The second question remains equally central, but it requires a more careful interpretation. In earlier stages of the project, protection was treated more directly through infrastructure-related datasets. The revised design is more cautious. It retains protection as an essential conceptual part of the thesis, because distributive flood justice cannot be evaluated only through hazard and exposure. At the same time, it acknowledges that protection is difficult to observe in a simple and spatially consistent way. The thesis therefore does not assume from the outset that protection can be represented as a straightforward municipality-level binary attribute. Instead, the role of protection is addressed in a staged manner: first by building the exposure-vulnerability comparison cleanly, and then by examining how protection-related differentiation may shape this relationship. This approach is analytically stronger than forcing a weak operationalization too early. It also corresponds to the broader flood-justice literature. De Goër de Herve (2022) emphasizes that justice claims in flood risk management require careful interpretation, while Johnson et al. (2007) show that uneven flood outcomes do not automatically distinguish between “natural” and socially imposed injustices.

Taken together, the two research questions define the thesis as a distributive inquiry rather than as a narrowly causal or engineering-based one. The empirical goal is to identify whether socially differentiated municipalities also experience systematically differentiated flood-related outcomes. If such a pattern exists, it becomes analytically meaningful in the language of justice. If it does not, this is equally informative, because it suggests that flood exposure and protection-related differentiation are not strongly aligned with the socio-economic geography of the corridor. In either case, the questions are designed to evaluate spatial patterning rather than to prove intent, moral fault, or a single deterministic mechanism. This point is important, because, as Rufat et al. (2019) argue, social vulnerability models are highly useful for structured comparison, but their explanatory reach should not be overstated.

## Conceptual Model

The conceptual model of this thesis is built around the triad of exposure, vulnerability, and protection. These three elements are analytically distinct, but closely connected. Exposure refers here to the degree to which municipal territory is affected by flooding under specified return periods. In the context of this study, exposure is operationalized through municipality-level flood shares derived from flood-depth rasters, which indicate how much of each municipality lies within modeled flood extent. Vulnerability, by contrast, refers to the social conditions that shape how seriously such exposure matters. It captures not the physical presence of water, but the differentiated capacity of municipalities to anticipate, cope with, and recover from flood impacts. Following Rufat et al. (2015), vulnerability is therefore treated as a multidimensional social condition rather than as a single trait. German work by Fekete (2009, 2010) is especially useful here, because it shows that social vulnerability to river floods can be operationalized at subnational scale and can reveal hidden strengths and weaknesses that remain invisible in purely physical flood analysis.

This triad is translated into the empirical design in a deliberately structured way. Exposure is represented through a multi-return-period flood dataset at municipality level, including `RP10`, `RP20`, `RP50`, `RP100`, `RP200`, and `RP500`. This allows the thesis to work not only with one single hazard snapshot, but with a broader exposure profile across return periods. For the main comparative analysis, `RP100` functions as the current benchmark, because it is both policy-relevant and analytically interpretable. Vulnerability is represented by a municipality-level socio-economic index derived from INKAR. Here, the thesis follows the broader vulnerability literature in treating vulnerability as multidimensional rather than reducible to one indicator such as income alone. Rufat et al. (2015) identify demographic characteristics, socio-economic status, and health as leading drivers of flood vulnerability, while also emphasizing that indicator selection should be tailored to the relevant context. For this reason, the vulnerability side of the thesis is built as a composite framework, but not as an uncritical one.

This immediately raises an important methodological issue. Vulnerability indices are useful because they make complex social patterns comparable across space, yet they are never neutral or self-evident. Rufat et al. (2019) note that most social vulnerability models are primarily descriptive and that their empirical validity cannot simply be assumed. Tate (2013) likewise shows that decisions about indicator selection, normalization, weighting, and scale introduce substantial uncertainty into any index construction. Schmidtlein et al. (2008) add that such models are sensitive to changes in scale and variable composition, especially when PCA-based approaches are used. For this thesis, this means that the vulnerability index is best understood as a structured analytical tool rather than as a perfect direct representation of social reality. This caution does not weaken the model. On the contrary, it is what makes the comparison transparent and interpretable.

Protection forms the third component of the conceptual model and is also the most difficult one. Flood-risk-management literature and socio-hydrological research both show that protection is never socially neutral. Tobin (1995) already argued that structural protection can create a false sense of security and encourage further development in flood-prone areas. Serra-Llobet et al. (2022) and Fu et al. (2023) make a similar point in the language of residual risk by emphasizing that protection can reduce frequent damage while leaving substantial risk in place when thresholds are exceeded or systems fail. For this reason, protection cannot be treated here as a simple background condition. Instead, it is understood as a historically produced and imperfect factor that shapes how physical hazard becomes actual exposure and remaining risk. This is consistent with socio-hydrological thinking, which, as Sivapalan et al. (2012) argue, treats exposure, adaptation, protection, and residual risk as co-evolving rather than independent layers.

Taken together, the conceptual model implies a straightforward but important empirical logic. If flood exposure is spatially uneven, and if socio-economic vulnerability is also spatially uneven, then justice-relevant questions arise where these two distributions overlap. More specifically, the thesis asks whether municipalities with higher social vulnerability also tend to carry greater flood exposure and, later, whether protection-related differentiation changes this pattern. In this sense, justice becomes analytically operational without being reduced to a simple moral label. Following de Goër de Herve (2022), the thesis treats distributive justice as a question of whether burdens and benefits are systematically aligned with social difference. At the same time, it does not assume that every uneven pattern automatically proves injustice. Rather, it identifies whether the geography of exposure and the geography of protection-related outcomes appear to align systematically with the geography of social vulnerability. That is the analytical task that links the conceptual discussion of Chapter 2 to the research design and empirical strategy of the chapters that follow.
