## Overall Approach

This thesis is designed as a spatially explicit, municipality-level study of flood exposure, socio-economic vulnerability, and protection-related differentiation in the Elbe context. It is not a purely hydrological flood-risk assessment, because its central interest is not only the physical extent of flooding. At the same time, it is not a purely institutional or governance study, because it seeks to identify empirical spatial patterns rather than reconstruct policy processes in depth. Instead, the thesis combines both perspectives through a distributive justice lens. In this sense, it examines whether flood-related burdens and protection-related advantages are distributed unevenly across municipalities with different socio-economic profiles, and whether these patterns are strong enough to raise justice-relevant concerns (de Goër de Herve, 2022; Poussard et al., 2021; Thaler & Hartmann, 2016).

The study remains conceptually anchored in the Elbe river basin, because the basin represents the broader hydrological, historical, and governance frame within which flood management, settlement development, and public intervention have evolved. Empirically, however, the analysis is narrowed to the flood-relevant Elbe corridor. This distinction is important. A basin-wide inclusion of municipalities would also capture places that are hydrologically connected to the Elbe system but not meaningfully relevant for the spatial comparison of flood exposure. The revised design therefore defines the study population through the extreme flood-relevant corridor rather than through the full basin alone. In practical terms, only municipalities intersecting the `RP500` flood extent are retained. This approach sharpens the analytical focus without abandoning the basin framing of the thesis. It follows the broader literature on flood-risk assessment, which has repeatedly shown that spatial scale and study extent are not neutral technical choices, but directly shape the patterns that can be observed and interpreted (de Moel et al., 2015; Poussard et al., 2021; Sairam et al., 2021).

Within this corridor-based sample, the thesis adopts a staged analytical strategy. The first step is to establish a robust exposure-vulnerability framework at municipality level. For that purpose, flood exposure is derived consistently across several return periods, while socio-economic vulnerability is represented through a multidimensional indicator framework based on INKAR. This creates the empirical basis for the core distributive question of the thesis: whether more socially vulnerable municipalities also tend to face greater flood exposure. The second step concerns protection. Protection remains conceptually central to the thesis and is therefore not removed from the analytical framework. However, the study does not treat protection as a simple binary state of “protected” versus “unprotected”, because both socio-hydrological research and flood-risk-management literature show that protection is partial, differentiated, and always accompanied by residual risk (Fu et al., 2023; Serra-Llobet et al., 2022; Sivapalan et al., 2012; Tobin, 1995). For this reason, the thesis first builds a strong exposure-vulnerability design and then extends the analysis toward protection-related interpretation in a more cautious and methodologically transparent way.

This overall approach follows directly from the theoretical logic developed in Chapter 2. If flood exposure is spatially uneven, if socio-economic vulnerability is spatially uneven, and if protection influences how physical hazard becomes actual exposure and remaining risk, then the relevant empirical task is to compare these geographies within a coherent analytical framework. The municipality is used as the key spatial unit because it allows the integration of flood exposure and socio-economic information within one comparable administrative scale, while still retaining enough spatial variation for a meaningful distributive analysis. The result is a research design that is explicitly comparative, justice-oriented, and spatially grounded, but also careful about the limits of what can be inferred from municipality-level patterns alone (How Valid Are Social Vulnerability Models?, 2019; Rufat et al., 2015).

## Research Questions

The thesis is guided by two fixed research questions:

1. How is flood hazard and exposure distributed across socio-economic groups in the Elbe River basin?
2. What role does flood protection infrastructure play in shaping this distribution?

Under the revised design, these questions remain unchanged, but they are addressed through a more sharply defined empirical strategy. The first question is approached directly through the municipality-level comparison of flood exposure and socio-economic vulnerability within the flood-relevant Elbe corridor. In this context, “socio-economic groups” are not observed as individuals or neighborhoods, but approximated through municipality-level social profiles. This means that the thesis does not claim to identify intra-municipal sorting or household-level inequality. Rather, it examines whether municipalities with different socio-economic characteristics systematically occupy different positions in the geography of flood exposure. This remains fully consistent with the question as stated, while also matching the scale at which the available data can be integrated coherently (de Moel et al., 2015; Odersky & Löffler, 2024; Poussard et al., 2021).

The second question remains equally central, but it requires a more careful interpretation. In the earlier stages of the project, protection was treated more directly through infrastructure-related datasets. The revised design is more cautious. It retains protection as an essential conceptual part of the thesis, because distributive flood justice cannot be evaluated only through hazard and exposure. At the same time, it acknowledges that protection is difficult to observe in a simple and spatially consistent way. As a result, the thesis does not assume from the outset that protection can be represented as a straightforward municipality-level binary attribute. Instead, the role of protection is addressed in a staged manner: first by building the exposure-vulnerability comparison cleanly, and then by interpreting how protection-related differences may shape this relationship. This approach is analytically stronger than forcing a weak operationalization too early. It also aligns with the broader flood-justice literature, which emphasizes that distributive claims should be made carefully and that uneven outcomes do not automatically reveal either political intent or a single underlying cause (de Goër de Herve, 2022; Johnson et al., 2007; Thaler & Hartmann, 2016).

Taken together, the two research questions define the thesis as a distributive inquiry rather than as a narrowly causal or engineering-based one. The empirical goal is to identify whether socially differentiated municipalities also experience systematically differentiated flood-related outcomes. If such a pattern exists, it becomes analytically meaningful in the language of justice. If it does not, this is equally informative, because it suggests that flood exposure and protection-related differentiation are not strongly aligned with the socio-economic geography of the corridor. In either case, the questions are designed to evaluate spatial patterning rather than to prove intent, moral fault, or a single deterministic mechanism (de Goër de Herve, 2022; How Valid Are Social Vulnerability Models?, 2019).

## Conceptual Model

The conceptual model of this thesis is built around the triad of exposure, vulnerability, and protection. These three elements are analytically distinct, but closely connected. Exposure refers here to the degree to which municipal territory is affected by flooding under specified return periods. In the context of this study, exposure is operationalized through municipality-level flood shares derived from flood-depth rasters, which indicate how much of each municipality lies within modeled flood extent. Vulnerability, by contrast, refers to the social conditions that shape how seriously such exposure matters. It captures not the physical presence of water, but the differentiated capacity of municipalities to anticipate, cope with, and recover from flood impacts. Protection, finally, refers to the arrangements that reduce, redirect, delay, or otherwise shape the translation of physical hazard into realized exposure and remaining risk. In this framework, protection is not equivalent to absolute safety. It is understood as a socially consequential and imperfect component of flood risk management (de Moel et al., 2015; Rufat et al., 2015; Sairam et al., 2021; Thaler & Hartmann, 2016).

This triad is translated into the empirical design in a deliberately structured way. Exposure is represented through a multi-return-period flood dataset at municipality level, including `RP10`, `RP20`, `RP50`, `RP100`, `RP200`, and `RP500`. This allows the thesis to work not only with one single hazard snapshot, but with a broader exposure profile across return periods. For the main comparative analysis, `RP100` functions as the current benchmark, because it is both policy-relevant and analytically interpretable. Vulnerability is represented by a municipality-level socio-economic index derived from INKAR. Here, the thesis follows the broader vulnerability literature in treating vulnerability as multidimensional rather than reducible to one indicator such as income alone. In particular, the literature suggests that demographic characteristics, socio-economic status, health-related conditions, and coping capacity tend to emerge repeatedly as important drivers of flood vulnerability, while also warning that indicator sets must be tailored to context and hazard type (Fekete, 2009, 2010; Rufat et al., 2015). The use of a composite vulnerability model is therefore justified, but it is also treated critically rather than as a neutral measurement device.

A central methodological implication follows from this. Vulnerability indices are useful because they make complex social patterns comparable across space, yet they are inevitably shaped by decisions about indicator selection, normalization, weighting, aggregation, and scale. The literature is very clear on this point. Social vulnerability models are often descriptive rather than strongly explanatory, and their empirical validity cannot simply be assumed (How Valid Are Social Vulnerability Models?, 2019). Likewise, index construction introduces uncertainty through a series of design choices, including indicator selection, scale of analysis, and weighting strategy (Tate, 2013). Results are also sensitive to changes in geographic scale and variable composition, especially when broad PCA-based approaches are used (Schmidtlein et al., 2008). For this thesis, this means that the vulnerability index is best understood as a structured analytical tool for comparing municipalities, not as a perfect direct representation of social reality. This perspective does not weaken the model. On the contrary, it provides the transparency needed to interpret the results responsibly.

Protection enters the conceptual model as the most difficult but also one of the most important elements. Flood-risk-management literature and socio-hydrological research both show that protection is never socially neutral. It changes where water is allowed to go, influences settlement development, affects perceptions of safety, and leaves residual risk in place when design thresholds are exceeded or protective systems fail (Fu et al., 2023; Serra-Llobet et al., 2022; Tobin, 1995). For this reason, protection cannot be treated here as a simple background condition. At the same time, the thesis avoids treating protection as a fully observed and technically unambiguous municipality-level variable where the underlying evidence is still evolving. The conceptual model therefore uses protection as a moderating and historically produced factor that shapes how hazard becomes lived risk, while the empirical design approaches its role in a staged and cautious way. This is fully consistent with socio-hydrological thinking, which emphasizes that exposure, adaptation, protection, and residual risk co-evolve rather than exist as independent layers (Sivapalan et al., 2012).

Taken together, the conceptual model implies a straightforward but important empirical logic. If flood exposure is spatially uneven, and if socio-economic vulnerability is also spatially uneven, then justice-relevant questions arise where these two distributions overlap. More specifically, the thesis asks whether municipalities with higher social vulnerability also tend to carry greater flood exposure and, later, whether protection-related differentiation changes this pattern. In this sense, justice becomes analytically operational without being reduced to a simple moral label. The thesis does not assume that every uneven map proves injustice. Rather, it identifies whether the burdens of exposure and the advantages of protection-related arrangements appear to align systematically with social vulnerability. That is the analytical task that links the conceptual discussion of Chapter 2 to the research design and empirical strategy of the chapters that follow (de Goër de Herve, 2022; Poussard et al., 2021; Thaler & Hartmann, 2016).
