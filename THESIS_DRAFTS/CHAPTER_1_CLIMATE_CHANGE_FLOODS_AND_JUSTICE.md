## Climate Change, Floods & Justice

Climate change does not only increase the urgency of flood adaptation. It also changes the terms on which adaptation is judged. As flood hazards intensify and public authorities face growing pressure to prevent losses, support recovery, and maintain protection standards, decisions about flood risk management become more consequential politically and socially. The literature on flood justice makes clear that these decisions cannot be evaluated by efficiency alone. They also raise questions of fairness, because adaptation measures allocate public resources, shape territorial priorities, and influence who receives more safety and who remains more exposed (de Goër de Herve, 2022; Thaler & Hartmann, 2016). In this sense, climate adaptation is not simply a technical matter of reducing expected damages. It is also a question of how risk reduction is distributed across places and social groups.

This is especially visible in the case of floods. Liao et al. (2019) argue that flood prevention can generate environmental injustice when interventions redistribute floodwater instead of removing it altogether. Johnson et al. (2007) similarly note that flood patterns are spatially uneven to begin with and that public interventions may either mitigate or reinforce these uneven outcomes. A justice perspective therefore does not require the assumption that all flood inequalities are intentionally produced. Rather, it starts from the observation that flood governance can create differentiated benefits and differentiated burdens. Broader environmental inequality research supports this view by showing that environmental harms are often linked to wider social inequalities and institutional asymmetries rather than being randomly distributed across society (Shao et al., 2022). Under conditions of climate change, this connection becomes more important, because adaptation decisions help determine which spaces are stabilized, which risks remain, and which populations may face a disproportionate share of the remaining burden.

For this reason, the present thesis approaches flood risk through a distributive justice lens. The central question is not whether every observed inequality can be traced back to a single policy decision. It is whether the spatial distribution of flood exposure and later protection-related patterns aligns with broader socio-economic inequalities in ways that should be treated as justice-relevant. Recent evidence from Germany points strongly in this direction. Odersky and Löffler (2024) show that the 2021 floods had a regressive distribution at the local level, while Tovar Reaños (2021) demonstrates that flood-related welfare losses weigh more heavily on lower-income households. Together, these findings suggest that climate-related flood impacts are socially differentiated both before and after an event. The deeper conceptual discussion of environmental and flood justice follows in Chapter 2. At the level of the introduction, however, one conclusion already follows: flood adaptation in a climate-changed future has to be evaluated not only by the amount of risk it reduces, but also by how that reduction is distributed.
