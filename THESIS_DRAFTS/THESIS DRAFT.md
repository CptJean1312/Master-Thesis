1.	Introduction
> "Floods are 'acts of God,' but flood losses are largely acts of man."  
> (White, 1945)

Motivation & Problem Framing
Flooding is among the most consequential environmental hazards of contemporary societies. As Jongman et al. (2012) note, flooding of coastal and fluvial systems affects several million people globally each year, while the population and value of assets located in flood-prone areas have increased substantially over recent decades. Their global assessment further suggests that economic exposure to river and coastal flooding has grown sharply and is likely to continue rising in the future. In other words, flood risk is not only a matter of extreme hydrometeorological events. It is also a product of how populations, infrastructures, and economic assets have become increasingly concentrated in vulnerable locations. This makes flooding a problem of both physical hazard and socio-economic development.

In Europe, this problem has become especially visible in the context of climate change and adaptation. Rojas et al. (2013) describe river flooding as one of the major climate-related risks facing the European Union and show that future damages may rise dramatically if current levels of protection are simply kept constant. Their analysis projects expected annual flood damages in the EU from around EUR 5.5 billion under current conditions to almost EUR 98 billion by the 2080s in a no-adaptation trajectory, while also showing that adaptation can substantially reduce these losses and may be highly cost-effective. For the present thesis, this shifts the question from whether flood risk matters to how it is governed. Once adaptation and protection become public tasks, the spatial distribution of their benefits and limits becomes analytically relevant. Flood risk management is therefore not only a technical response to hazard. It is part of a broader political and social negotiation over future safety, acceptable losses, and territorial priorities.

Germany offers a particularly strong setting in which to examine these issues, because major flood events have repeatedly triggered policy change and public debate. Thieken et al. (2016) identify the 2002 flood as a decisive turning point in the German shift toward integrated flood risk management, while the 2013 event revealed both the continuing severity of flood losses and the role of institutional learning. Within this national context, the Elbe stands out as one of the most visible and analytically relevant river corridors. Grabs (2016) shows that the extreme Elbe floods of 2002 and 2013 triggered the Elbe Flood Protection Action Plan and became a benchmark for broader debates on integrated flood risk reduction. The Elbe is therefore not only a hydrological space, but also a historically significant corridor of settlement, intervention, and policy learning. It is precisely this combination of repeated flood experience, extensive protection efforts, and wider governance relevance that makes the Elbe a compelling case for studying the distribution of flood-related burdens and benefits.

At the same time, the significance of flooding cannot be understood adequately if it is reduced to aggregate damage figures alone. Flood impacts are socially differentiated. Tovar Reaños (2021) shows for Germany that expected flood damages affect households differently across income groups and that welfare losses after floods disproportionately harm low-income households. More recent evidence by Odersky and Löffler (2024) points in the same direction from another angle. Using actual damage data from the 2021 floods in Germany, they show that flood exposure was regressive at the local level, with poorer neighborhoods displaying substantially higher exposure than better-off nearby areas. These findings matter because they suggest that flood-related harm is not distributed randomly across space or society. Exposure, coping capacity, and recovery potential are shaped by socio-economic conditions, which means that flood governance also has a social distributional dimension.

This is the practical puzzle from which this thesis starts. If flood risk is growing, if adaptation and protection are becoming more central, and if flood-related harms are socially differentiated, then the key issue is no longer only how much flood risk exists. It is also who is exposed, who benefits from risk reduction, and whether these patterns align with broader socio-economic inequalities. The Elbe provides a particularly suitable setting for asking these questions because it combines a long history of flood events and protection strategies with marked spatial variation in local social conditions. The thesis therefore begins from the assumption that flood exposure and protection should not be treated as socially neutral outcomes. Rather, they need to be examined as potentially uneven and justice-relevant spatial patterns that require systematic analysis.

Under climate change, this puzzle becomes even more pressing because adaptation decisions are not judged by efficiency alone. As de Goër de Herve (2022) and Thaler and Hartmann (2016) show, flood risk management also raises questions of fairness, because protection measures allocate public resources and shape who receives greater safety and who remains more exposed. Liao et al. (2019) further argue that flood prevention can generate environmental injustice when interventions redistribute floodwater rather than removing it altogether. For this reason, the thesis approaches flood risk through a distributive justice lens, while the deeper conceptual discussion of environmental and flood justice is developed in Chapter 2.

RESEARCH GAP & THESIS CONTRIBUTION
The literature on floods, vulnerability, and flood risk management is already substantial, and this thesis does not start from an empty field. A large body of work has examined hydrological hazard, damage estimation, governance reform, and the social dimensions of vulnerability. In parallel, a growing international literature has shown that flood exposure is often socially uneven rather than randomly distributed. Qiang (2019) demonstrates this for the United States at national scale, while Poussard et al. (2021) show that environmental inequalities in flood exposure are strongly shaped by the spatial scale at which they are measured. More recent work for Latin America reaches a similar conclusion and stresses that substantial social disparities in flood exposure can also be identified at the local level (Kephart et al., 2025). German research has also moved in this direction. Tovar Reaños (2021) links flood damages to inequality in welfare terms, and Odersky and Löffler (2024) provide evidence that flood exposure in Germany was regressive at the local scale during the 2021 event. Taken together, these studies make clear that the relationship between flooding and social inequality is no longer a marginal topic. It has become an increasingly visible research field in its own right.

At the same time, an important gap remains. Existing studies often focus on one dimension in relative isolation: physical hazard and damage, social vulnerability measurement, or governance and protection strategies. These strands are not always brought together within one coherent distributive framework. This is especially relevant because flood inequality is, as Poussard et al. (2021) argue, a matter of scale. Patterns that appear weak or invisible at broader regional levels can become much clearer when the analysis moves to smaller spatial units. Older German work already pointed to this problem. Fekete (2010) argued that an overview of social vulnerability to river floods in Germany was missing and emphasized that only few studies had addressed the non-structural and socially differentiated dimensions of flood risk in a systematic way. Rufat et al. (2015) add a broader methodological caution by showing that there remains a large gap between the contextual complexity of social vulnerability and the generalized indicator practices often used to measure it. Rufat et al. (2019) make a similar point by arguing that many social vulnerability models remain primarily descriptive. For the purposes of this thesis, the implication is not that previous work is inadequate. Rather, it is that there is still limited municipality-level evidence that directly links flood exposure patterns with socio-economic vulnerability in a justice-oriented way for the Elbe corridor.

This is where the present thesis is positioned. Its contribution lies in combining a municipality-based exposure analysis with a socio-economic vulnerability perspective in one analytically coherent framework. Empirically, the thesis focuses on the Elbe flood corridor and examines how flood exposure is distributed across municipalities with different social characteristics. In doing so, it responds directly to the scale problem identified in the literature and to the need for more spatially explicit evidence on distributive patterns of flood risk. At the same time, it builds on the German vulnerability literature without simply reproducing it. The thesis does not only map socio-economic vulnerability, nor does it restrict itself to hazard or damage alone. Instead, it links flood exposure to a multidimensional vulnerability index and keeps the broader question of protection conceptually central to the analysis of unequal flood-related outcomes. The contribution is therefore both empirical and conceptual: it adds new municipality-level evidence for the Elbe corridor and situates that evidence within the broader debate on flood justice, socio-economic vulnerability, and spatial inequality.

RESEARCH QUESTIONS & ANALYTICAL ORIENTATION
1. How is modeled flood exposure distributed across municipalities with different socio-economic vulnerability profiles in the Elbe flood corridor?
2. How do modeled protection and loss-related outcomes relate to flood exposure and socio-economic vulnerability across this corridor?

These questions are approached as distributive and spatial questions. The analysis does not observe individual households or neighborhood sorting directly, but compares municipalities within the flood-relevant Elbe corridor as socially differentiated local contexts. The aim is therefore to identify whether municipalities with different socio-economic profiles also occupy systematically different positions in the geography of flood exposure, modeled loss occurrence, annual loss probability, and finite protection return periods.

Accordingly, the thesis does not seek to infer policy intent or to establish a single causal mechanism behind observed inequalities. Instead, it evaluates whether the overlap between socio-economic vulnerability and flood-related outcomes is strong enough to raise distributive justice concerns and to merit further interpretation in the context of German flood risk management.

The wording of the thesis title is used in this operational sense. The title refers to the spatial distribution of flood protection infrastructure across socially relevant factors in the Elbe river basin. Empirically, the thesis does not provide an inventory of individual dikes, flood walls, or technical protection assets. Instead, protection infrastructure is examined through modeled spatial effects: positive modeled loss occurrence, annual loss probability, finite protection return periods, and no-event municipalities in the provider loss/protection portfolio. These outcomes are interpreted together with multi-return-period exposure curves, because they show how protection, residual risk, and model scope shape whether flood hazard becomes visible as modeled loss. The socially relevant factors are represented by the INKAR-based vulnerability dimensions, while the Elbe river basin remains the broader hydrological and governance frame within which the RP500 flood-relevant corridor is analyzed.

2. Theoretical & Conceptual Background
Flood Risk Management in Germany
Flood risk management in Germany has changed substantially over the last decades. While earlier approaches were dominated by technical flood protection and the attempt to control water mainly through structural measures, more recent debates and policy developments increasingly frame flooding as a broader risk management problem. This shift was not purely conceptual, but strongly event-driven. According to Thieken et al. (2016), the 2002 flood marked an important reorientation toward integrated flood risk management in Germany. A similar point is made by Sairam et al. (2021), who note that the major events of 1993, 1995, and especially 2002 triggered policy decisions in favor of more systemic approaches. In this context, the European Floods Directive became a key institutional milestone, because it required member states to identify significant flood risks and prepare flood risk management plans. Germany implemented this broader orientation within its national legal and planning framework, which means that flood risk management today has to be understood as a combination of hazard assessment, spatial planning, emergency management, damage reduction, and long-term adaptation rather than as flood defense alone (Sairam et al., 2021; Thieken et al., 2016).

This shift becomes clearer when considering the role of major flood events in German policy learning. Thieken et al. (2016) describe the 2002 flood as the most expensive natural hazard event in Germany up to that point, with damage of EUR 11.6 billion and 21 fatalities. The 2013 flood again caused very high losses, estimated at EUR 6 to 8 billion, but it also demonstrated that some improvements had already taken effect. Thieken et al. (2016) argue that by 2013 Germany had improved flood warnings, disaster response coordination, property-level mitigation, and the maintenance of flood defense systems. In a similar way, Grabs (2016) presents the Elbe floods of 2002 and 2013 as key triggers for the Elbe Flood Protection Action Plan and as a major moment in the broader acceptance of integrated flood risk management. These events therefore matter not only because they caused severe damage, but because they exposed weaknesses in existing institutions and made flood governance a long-term political issue. Germany is thus a useful case because its current flood risk management system emerged through repeated extreme events, institutional learning, and ongoing policy adjustment.

A central characteristic of German flood risk management is that it operates across multiple governance levels and across different types of measures. At the European level, the Floods Directive created a common framework, while national and subnational actors remain responsible for implementation, planning, and financing in practice (Sairam et al., 2021). This multi-level structure means that flood risk management outcomes are shaped through coordination problems, regional variation, and institutional capacity, not only through technical necessity. At the same time, the German debate has moved beyond a narrow focus on dikes and flood walls. Grabs (2016) emphasizes that integrated flood management includes a mix of structural and non-structural measures, including warning systems, land-use regulation, reactivation of former floodplains, and broader adaptation strategies. Thieken et al. (2016) likewise stress the importance of spatial planning, preparedness, and public involvement. More recent work also shows that flood risk management increasingly includes private and semi-private dimensions, such as insurance, household mitigation, and municipality-level communication. Surminski and Thieken (2017) argue that insurance should be seen as part of a broader anticipatory flood risk management field, while Osberghaus (2015) shows that private mitigation remains important because public protection cannot eliminate flood risk entirely.

Despite this broader framing, German flood risk management still contains important tensions. On the one hand, public policy increasingly recognizes that absolute protection is neither technically nor economically feasible. Grabs (2016) explicitly argues that structural measures can mitigate flood hazards but cannot eliminate them, and that relying on engineering solutions alone may even increase residual risk and vulnerability in the event of failure. On the other hand, structural protection remains politically central because it is visible, state-led, and strongly associated with public safety. This creates a persistent tension between reactive and anticipatory approaches. Surminski and Thieken (2017) describe flood risk management in Germany and England as still largely reactive and event-driven, even though anticipatory approaches are widely discussed. Recent research on household adaptation in Germany supports this interpretation. Osberghaus (2015) and Dillenardt and Thieken (2025) both show that private adaptation, communication, and individual preparedness are important, but unevenly developed. In other words, integrated flood risk management has expanded conceptually, yet in practice it remains unevenly realized and still heavily shaped by crisis response, historical legacies, and institutional path dependencies.

For this thesis, the German context is therefore relevant in two ways. First, it provides an institutional setting in which flood risk management is formally broad enough to include hazard reduction, vulnerability reduction, planning, and preparedness. Second, it remains a setting in which structural protection infrastructure continues to play a major political and practical role. This duality matters for a study that examines the distribution of flood exposure and flood protection in the Elbe context. If German flood risk management is both integrated in principle and uneven in practice, then questions of distribution become unavoidable. The relevant issue is not only whether protection exists, but how its benefits and the remaining burdens of exposure are distributed across different municipalities and socio-economic conditions. In this sense, Germany is not merely the national background of the thesis, but the governance context that makes the central research questions analytically meaningful.

AREA OF INTEREST
The Elbe is one of the major river systems in Germany and has long been a central space of flood risk management, settlement development, and political intervention. In the context of this thesis, the Elbe is not treated merely as a hydrological basin, but as a socio-spatial corridor in which flood dynamics, settlement patterns, and public protection strategies intersect. Repeated major flood events have made the Elbe one of the most visible river systems in German flood policy. Grabs (2016) highlights the Elbe floods of 2002 and 2013 as key triggers for the Elbe Flood Protection Action Plan, while Jüpner (2018) underlines that the Elbe flood disaster of 2013 again demonstrated both the destructive potential of extreme floods and the limits of technical protection. In this sense, the Elbe is not just an empirically convenient case. It is a river corridor in which the relationships between hazard, protection, and social conditions can be observed especially clearly.

Historically, the Elbe has been shaped by a long interaction between settlement, river engineering, and flood experience. Jüpner (2018) shows that settlement along the Elbe originally concentrated on relatively elevated areas near the river, as in Magdeburg, where settlement historically remained more than 15 m above mean river level. Over time, however, population growth, land-use pressure, and hydraulic engineering transformed the river into an increasingly engineered system. According to Jüpner (2018), more than 500 km of the Elbe in Germany are now almost completely lined by dikes, and more than 83% of the natural floodplains along the river have been lost. For the present study, this illustrates that the Elbe is not a “natural” floodplain in any simple sense. It is a heavily modified river landscape in which exposure, protection, and residual risk are historically co-produced. Grabs (2016) makes a similar point by arguing that integrated flood management in the Elbe context must move beyond technical flood control alone and combine structural and non-structural strategies. The Elbe therefore offers an especially useful setting for examining how flood exposure and flood protection are embedded in a broader socio-political landscape.

The Elbe is also a strong case because it has repeatedly been affected by severe flood events in recent decades. The major floods of 2002 and 2013 are especially relevant in this regard, but they are not the only events that matter. Barendrecht et al. (2018) discuss the Dresden case and show that the damage in 2013 was lower than in 2002 even though flood risk remained substantial, which they interpret as evidence that societal awareness and preparedness had changed after the earlier event. Jüpner (2018) likewise argues that the 2013 flood reopened debates on how flood risk should be managed, especially because two dike failures occurred and because areas assumed to be protected still suffered major damage. Earlier work by Lindenschmidt et al. (2006) further shows that large-basin risk assessment in the Elbe and Mulde context is methodologically demanding and was, at that time, still in its infancy. Together, these studies show that the Elbe is characterized not only by repeated flood exposure, but also by repeated institutional and social learning. The area of interest is therefore not simply flood-prone; it is a landscape in which flood events have continuously reshaped risk perception, protection strategies, and governance debates.

At the same time, the Elbe corridor is not only hydrologically heterogeneous, but also socially and economically uneven. This matters for the logic of the thesis because the river crosses regions that are embedded in broader East-West and urban-rural disparities within Germany. The 2024 report *Ost und West. Frei, vereint und unvollkommen* emphasizes that the social and economic transformation of Germany after reunification remains incomplete and that eastern and western parts of the country still differ in structural respects (Ostdeutschland, 2024). A similar picture emerges from the broader regional disparity literature. Heider et al. (2023) argue that existing socio-economic disparities may intensify if effective preventive measures are lacking and stress the role of reliable indicators for identifying emerging patterns of inequality. Helbig (2023), in turn, shows that socio-spatial inequality in German cities is expressed through persistent concentrations of poverty, wealth, education, and social segregation. For the purposes of this thesis, these sources show that the Elbe corridor runs through municipalities with very different social and economic profiles. The study area therefore cannot be understood only in terms of river morphology or floodplains. It must also be understood as a socially differentiated space in which vulnerability and adaptive capacity are unevenly distributed.

This socio-spatial heterogeneity is one of the main reasons why the Elbe is particularly suitable for a justice-oriented flood study. A flood-prone corridor that crosses municipalities with differing income structures, household profiles, demographic characteristics, and accessibility conditions creates a setting in which distributive questions become highly relevant. The issue is not only whether flood hazard exists, but whether exposure and protection are distributed in ways that align with or contradict broader social inequalities. Odersky and Löffler (2024) show for the 2021 floods in Germany that flood exposure can be clearly regressive at the local level, with affected neighborhoods displaying substantially lower average incomes than nearby unaffected ones. Although their study focuses on a different flood event and spatial setup, it reinforces the broader relevance of distributional analysis in Germany. Fekete (2010) similarly argues that social vulnerability analysis can reveal hidden strengths and weaknesses of society in relation to river floods, a dimension only few German studies had directly addressed at that time. Against this background, the Elbe becomes analytically valuable not simply because it is flood-prone, but because it combines repeated flood experience, a long history of structural intervention, and a socially differentiated regional setting. This combination makes it particularly well suited for investigating how flood hazard, exposure, and protection relate to socially relevant factors in the Elbe river basin.

One final conceptual clarification is needed here. While the thesis remains framed in terms of the Elbe river basin, the empirical analysis later focuses on the flood-relevant corridor along the Elbe rather than on the full basin as a uniform analytical space. Not all municipalities that are hydrologically part of the basin are equally relevant for flood exposure analysis. The broader basin framing remains useful at the conceptual level because it anchors the study in the long-standing governance, planning, and river-management context of the Elbe as a major German river system. In other words, the Elbe basin provides the historical and institutional frame, whereas the corridor provides the empirically flood-relevant space. This combination allows the thesis to remain faithful to its title and research questions while still focusing analytically on the municipalities where flood hazard, exposure, and potential protection effects are most meaningful to compare.

ENVIRONMENTAL & FLOOD JUSTICE
Environmental justice provides the broader normative lens for this thesis because it shifts attention from floods as purely physical events to floods as socially differentiated forms of harm. In the general environmental inequality literature, the basic claim is that environmental burdens are not shared evenly across social groups and places, and that this unevenness is closely related to differences in economic status, political power, and institutional capacity (Shao et al., 2022). This perspective is directly relevant for flood research, even though the flood field has often been dominated by hydrological and engineering approaches. Floods may be triggered by hydrometeorological processes, but the question of who is exposed, who is protected, and who is left with the remaining risk is never purely natural. In this sense, flood risk becomes a justice issue once it is understood as the outcome of both physical processes and social hierarchies. Liao et al. (2019) make this point especially clearly by arguing that flood prevention should not be treated as a neutral technical exercise, because it does not eliminate floodwater but redistributes it. Protection for one area can therefore imply additional burdens for another. For the present thesis, the spatial distribution of flood exposure and the spatial distribution of protection-related benefits must therefore be read as socially meaningful patterns rather than as hydrological facts alone.

Within this broader frame, flood justice refers more specifically to the fairness of how flood-related burdens, benefits, and decision-making processes are organized. A key insight of the recent literature is that justice in flood risk management has several dimensions. de Goër de Herve (2022), in a review of justice considerations in flood risk management, shows that distributive and procedural justice are the two most frequently discussed dimensions, while spatial, temporal, and ecological concerns are often less systematically addressed. Johnson et al. (2007) similarly argue that flooding is “not fair per se” because flood frequency and flood extent are inherently uneven across space, while public interventions may either reduce or reinforce these inequalities. Thaler (2021) adds an important critique to current resilience debates by showing that resilience-oriented flood policy often focuses on efficiency and recovery, but can overlook pre-disaster inequalities and the uneven distribution of burdens within communities. A further element is recognition. Liao et al. (2019) stress that injustice does not arise only from unequal outcomes, but also from the failure to acknowledge marginalized communities as legitimate stakeholders in flood-related decision making. Taken together, this literature suggests that flood justice involves at least three connected questions: who bears flood exposure, who benefits from protective interventions, and whose interests are recognized when these outcomes are shaped. In this thesis, the main empirical emphasis lies on distributive justice, but this narrower focus is informed by the wider procedural and recognition debates.

The distributive dimension matters because flood risk management always allocates safety, insecurity, and public resources across space. Thaler and Hartmann (2016) formulate this very directly by asking who or what should be protected against inundation. Their argument shows that flood protection is not merely a technical response to hazard, but also a political decision about the allocation of resources, acceptable losses, and territorial priorities. O’Hare and White (2018) extend this line of thinking by proposing the concept of flood disadvantage. They argue that flooding interacts with broader social disadvantages across multiple policy domains and scales, and that technical flood risk analyses often struggle to capture these intersecting burdens because they tend to treat people and communities as overly homogeneous. In a similar way, Moulds et al. (2021) show that flood risk management can, under conditions of weak governance and strong inequality, reproduce or even intensify existing poverty and inequality rather than reduce them. These studies are especially useful for the present thesis because they make clear that justice is not only about the presence of hazard. It is also about whether the social benefits of flood management are distributed in ways that reduce disadvantage or, conversely, leave already vulnerable groups with a disproportionate share of residual risk.

A more critical reading of flood protection also requires asking how safety becomes tied to land values, settlement development, and the uneven capacity of places to attract political attention and investment. This does not mean that protection decisions can be reduced to market logic alone. It does mean, however, that protection is never only a hydraulic problem. Where valuable assets, dense infrastructure, administrative capacity, and political visibility are concentrated, flood risk is more likely to become a planning, insurance, and investment priority. Less visible or less asset-rich places may remain exposed through residual risk, weaker representation, or model boundaries that make their risks harder to see. The present thesis cannot reconstruct the history of protection investment or trace capital flows directly. It therefore does not make a causal claim about deliberate redistribution. However, it treats protection and modeled loss visibility as outcomes embedded in socio-economic and territorial priorities, which is consistent with critical flood-justice work on protection, disadvantage, and inequality (Thaler and Hartmann, 2016; O’Hare and White, 2018; Moulds et al., 2021; Tovar Reaños, 2021).

This perspective is supported by an increasing body of empirical work showing that flood exposure is often socially uneven. Fielding (2012), for example, demonstrates for England and Wales that inequalities in flood exposure can coincide with inequalities in flood awareness, so that the most deprived groups may also be the least aware of the risks they face. Qiang (2019) finds comparable socio-economic disparities in the United States and shows that disadvantaged populations are more likely to reside in flood zones at the national scale, although local patterns vary between inland and coastal areas. Poussard et al. (2021) add an important methodological point by showing that environmental inequalities in flood exposure are a matter of scale. Their findings suggest that the relationship between socio-economic status and flood exposure can look different depending on whether it is examined at larger regional scales or at finer local scales. This is highly relevant for the present thesis because it supports the decision to work at municipality level and to treat the spatial scale of analysis as a substantive issue rather than as a purely technical one. For Germany specifically, the findings of Odersky and Löffler (2024) are particularly important. They show that the 2021 floods in Germany had a clearly regressive distribution at the local level, with lower-income neighborhoods experiencing substantially higher exposure than nearby better-off areas. Taken together, these studies suggest that flood exposure cannot be assumed to be socially neutral, and that fine-scale empirical analysis is necessary to reveal its distributive structure.

A justice perspective also requires going beyond exposure alone and considering social vulnerability. The social vulnerability literature shows that similar levels of physical flood exposure can produce very different social consequences depending on the characteristics and capacities of the affected population. Rufat et al. (2015), in a meta-analysis of flood case studies, identify demographic characteristics, socio-economic status, and health as leading drivers of social vulnerability, while also emphasizing the importance of coping capacity and context. This matters for flood justice because unequal exposure does not exhaust the problem. Even where exposure is similar, poorer or otherwise disadvantaged groups may have fewer resources to prepare for, absorb, and recover from flood impacts. Tovar Reaños (2021) shows this clearly for Germany, arguing that welfare losses from floods disproportionately harm low-income households and can increase inequality. The implications are straightforward for the logic of this thesis: a distributive assessment of flood justice should not compare hazard patterns with social conditions in an isolated way, but should consider how socially differentiated vulnerability shapes the meaning and consequences of exposure.

Against this background, environmental and flood justice serve in this thesis as a conceptual bridge between the physical geography of flooding and the social geography of inequality. The empirical goal is not to prove political intent, nor to provide a full evaluation of procedural fairness in flood governance. Instead, the thesis asks whether the spatial distribution of flood exposure and protection/loss-related outcomes aligns with the spatial distribution of socio-economic vulnerability in ways that raise distributive justice concerns. This is a more modest claim, but also a more defensible one. As de Goër de Herve (2022) emphasizes, flood risk management involves competing interpretations of fairness, difficult trade-offs, and territorially uneven consequences. What can be examined empirically, however, is whether more socially vulnerable municipalities also tend to carry higher exposure or weaker apparent protection-related outcomes. In that sense, environmental and flood justice do not function here as abstract moral rhetoric. They provide the conceptual logic for why comparing exposure, vulnerability, and protection-related outcomes across municipalities is analytically meaningful in the first place.

SOCIO-HYDROLOGY AND HUMAN DIMENSION OF RISK
Flood risk cannot be understood adequately if it is treated as a purely physical interaction between discharge, topography, and inundation depth. This is the central insight of socio-hydrology. Sivapalan et al. (2012) define socio-hydrology as the study of coupled human-water systems and their co-evolution over time. The key point is that hydrological processes and social processes do not simply run in parallel. They interact through feedbacks. Human settlement, land use, technical protection, institutional learning, memory of past events, and changing expectations all affect flood risk, while flood events in turn alter behavior, policy, and spatial development. In this sense, flood losses are not determined by hazard alone, but by the long-term co-production of hazard, exposure, vulnerability, and adaptation. For this thesis, socio-hydrology provides the conceptual basis for linking flood exposure patterns to socio-economic structure. It suggests that the social geography of municipalities is not an external add-on to flood analysis, but part of the dynamics through which risk is produced and reproduced.

This argument is particularly relevant in river systems with long histories of settlement and protection. Socio-hydrological research has repeatedly shown that structural protection can reduce frequent flood damage in the short term while simultaneously contributing to higher long-term exposure and residual risk. Tobin (1995), in a classic critique of the “levee love affair”, argues that levees can generate a false sense of security, reduce preparedness, and encourage further development in areas that remain fundamentally flood-prone. More recent work has refined this insight. Ferdous et al. (2020) show for the Jamuna River in Bangladesh that higher levels of structural protection are associated with greater population growth and asset concentration in protected areas, while mortality can remain high when rare or extreme events occur. Serra-Llobet et al. (2022) similarly emphasize that even where levees are in place, a residual risk remains due to overtopping or breach, and that this residual risk must be incorporated into regulation, communication, and land-use planning. Fu et al. (2023) underline the same problem in the context of Aotearoa-New Zealand, where protective structures can encourage development in flood-prone areas and make the remaining risk politically difficult to manage. These studies are highly relevant here because they show that protection is not simply the opposite of risk. Rather, it can reshape where people live, how they perceive danger, and how much loss becomes possible when protection proves insufficient.

The socio-hydrological perspective also helps explain why similar hydrological events can produce different social outcomes over time. This is especially clear in research on the Elbe. Barendrecht et al. (2018) describe the Dresden case as one in which the 2013 flood caused lower damage than the 2002 flood, even though the later event was severe and the system had remained flood-prone. Their explanation is not that the hazard disappeared, but that awareness and preparedness changed after the earlier event. More generally, Kreibich et al. (2017), in their analysis of paired flood events across different countries, show that lower damage in later events is often linked to reduced vulnerability, for example through better awareness, preparedness, improved emergency management, and in some cases strengthened structural protection. Their results demonstrate that successful adaptation is often less a story of full hazard control than of changed societal response. The same event may therefore have different consequences depending on the experience, preparedness, and institutional learning that precede it. This supports a core idea of the thesis: flood risk is dynamic and partly socially learned, which means that it cannot be interpreted exclusively through static hazard maps.

Once the human dimension of risk is taken seriously, questions of individual and household behavior also become central. Public protection remains important, but it cannot eliminate flood risk completely. Osberghaus (2015) shows for Germany that private mitigation measures are an essential part of modern flood risk management precisely because public protection cannot fully prevent damage. His nationwide survey further indicates that mitigation behavior increases with flood experience and expectations of future damage. Bubeck et al. (2013) provide a complementary psychological perspective by showing that risk perception alone is often not enough to explain precautionary action. What matters strongly is coping appraisal, especially whether households believe that protective measures are effective, feasible, and worth the effort. Recent German evidence by Dillenardt and Thieken (2025) points in the same direction. Across different flood types, they find that the perceived effectiveness of adaptive measures and a stronger sense of personal responsibility are key drivers of adaptive behavior, while communication at the municipal level can strengthen these perceptions. This body of work shows that the human dimension of flood risk is not reducible to exposure alone. It also depends on knowledge, expectations, experience, trust, responsibility, and perceived room for action.

At the same time, the social consequences of repeated flood experience are not straightforwardly positive. Repeated events may strengthen preparedness in some respects, but they can also produce fatigue, unequal recovery, and psychological strain. Kuhlicke et al. (2020), drawing on three surveys of households and companies affected by the 2013 flood in Germany, show that repeated flood experience may increase resilience in relation to some material impacts, while evidence for psychological and health-related resilience is much more mixed and may even point to erosion. This is a useful corrective to simplified narratives of “learning by disaster”. Experience can trigger adaptation, but it does not automatically generate stronger resilience in every dimension. Some households may become better prepared materially, while still facing long-term emotional or financial stress. In this sense, the human dimension of risk is multidimensional: it includes material losses, behavioral responses, institutional support, and subjective capacities to withstand and recover.

For the present thesis, the main implication of socio-hydrology and the human-dimension literature is that flood exposure should not be interpreted as a purely physical outcome that can be analyzed independently of social structure. Exposure patterns are shaped by long-term feedbacks between settlement, protection, memory, adaptation, and governance. At the same time, the social consequences of exposure depend on vulnerability, preparedness, and recovery capacity. This is why it is meaningful to analyze municipal flood exposure together with socio-economic vulnerability. Doing so does not mean claiming that municipal social structure directly “causes” the hydrology of the Elbe. Rather, it recognizes that flood risk emerges from coupled social and hydrological processes, and that the benefits and limits of protection must be understood within this broader human-water system. This logic is especially important for the protection-related part of the thesis, because it clarifies that protection cannot be treated as a purely technical layer added on top of an otherwise social-neutral floodplain. It is itself part of the feedback system through which exposure, settlement, awareness, and residual risk evolve over time.


CONCEPTUAL FRAMING OF THIS STUDY
The previous sections suggest that this thesis is best understood through a conceptual triad of exposure, vulnerability, and protection. These three elements are analytically distinct, but closely connected. Exposure refers here to the spatial distribution of municipalities that are affected by flooding. In other words, it captures where the flood hazard actually intersects with inhabited and governed space. Vulnerability, by contrast, refers to the social conditions that shape how severely these exposures matter. Following Rufat et al. (2015), social vulnerability is not a single attribute, but a multidimensional set of characteristics related especially to socio-economic status, demographic structure, health, and coping capacity. German work by Fekete (2009, 2010) is particularly useful here because it shows that social vulnerability analysis can reveal hidden weaknesses and strengths of society in relation to river floods and can be operationalized with socio-demographic indicators at subnational scale. Protection, finally, is understood in this thesis not as absolute safety, but as the set of structural and risk-reducing arrangements that shape how much of the physical hazard is translated into actual flood exposure and remaining risk. Its social significance lies less in technical presence alone than in how protection redistributes safety, exposure, and residual risk across municipalities.

Taken together, these three elements provide the conceptual backbone of the study. The thesis does not ask only where flooding occurs, nor only which municipalities are socially vulnerable in a general sense. Instead, it asks how the spatial distribution of flood-related outcomes relates to the spatial distribution of socio-economic vulnerability, and whether this relationship points to unequal burdens or unequal benefits. In this sense, the justice perspective developed earlier becomes operational. Justice in this thesis is understood primarily in a distributive sense. Following Thaler and Hartmann (2016) and de Goër de Herve (2022), the relevant question is whether resources, risks, and protection-related advantages are distributed in a way that systematically favors or disadvantages particular social settings. This does not mean that every uneven pattern automatically proves injustice in a strong normative sense, and it does not imply a direct claim about political intent. Rather, the thesis uses distributive justice as an analytical lens: if more socially vulnerable municipalities systematically face higher flood exposure, or weaker protection-related benefits, this raises a justice-relevant pattern that deserves explanation.

This framing also implies a particular understanding of vulnerability measurement. Social vulnerability models are useful because they make multidimensional social conditions visible and comparable across space, but they are not neutral or self-evident. Rufat et al. (2019) note that most social vulnerability models are primarily descriptive and that their empirical validity cannot simply be assumed. Similarly, Tate (2013) shows that decisions about indicator selection, normalization, weighting, and scale introduce substantial uncertainty into any index construction, while Schmidtlein et al. (2008) demonstrate that such models are sensitive to changes in scale, variable selection, and geographic context. These methodological cautions are highly relevant for this thesis. They imply that a vulnerability index should be treated as a structured analytical tool rather than as a direct measure of some fixed social reality. At the same time, this does not weaken the usefulness of the concept. On the contrary, it means that vulnerability must be approached transparently, contextually, and with sensitivity to scale. This is one of the reasons why the thesis works at municipality level and relies on a multidimensional set of socio-economic indicators rather than on a single social marker such as income alone.

A similar caution applies to the concept of protection. The literature reviewed above shows that protection is socially consequential but never absolute. It can reduce frequent losses, influence settlement decisions, generate a sense of security, and leave residual risk in place when rare events exceed design standards or when protective systems fail (Fu et al., 2023; Serra-Llobet et al., 2022; Tobin, 1995). For the conceptual logic of this thesis, this means that protection should not be treated as a binary state of “safe” versus “unsafe”. It is better understood as a differentiated and imperfect component of flood risk management that shapes exposure patterns and their social consequences. This broader perspective matters because the empirical operationalization of protection can vary depending on data availability. The conceptual argument of the thesis, however, remains stable: protection is analytically relevant because it influences how hazard becomes lived and distributed risk.

The resulting framework can therefore be summarized in a simple way. Flood exposure is spatially uneven. Social vulnerability is multidimensional and socially structured. Protection is socially consequential, historically produced, and imperfect. A justice-oriented flood analysis must therefore compare the geography of exposure and protection-related outcomes with the geography of social vulnerability. This is precisely the role of the empirical chapters that follow. Chapter 3 translates this conceptual framing into the research design and analytical strategy, while Chapter 4 specifies the data and methods used to operationalize the three components in the Elbe context.

3. RESEARCH DESIGN & ANALYTICAL FRAMEWORK (5-7pages)
Overall Approach
Building on the theoretical and conceptual discussion of Chapter 2, this thesis adopts a spatially explicit municipality-level design for the analysis of flood exposure and socio-economic vulnerability in the Elbe context. The study is conceived as a distributive comparison rather than as a purely hydrological assessment or a purely institutional analysis. Its central aim is to identify whether municipalities with different socio-economic profiles also occupy systematically different positions in the geography of flood exposure and protection/loss-related outcomes. This general orientation follows the broader literature on environmental inequalities in flood exposure, which stresses that the relationship between social differentiation and flood hazard is meaningful only when it is examined at an appropriate spatial scale and within a clearly defined study setting (Poussard et al., 2021).

Within this corridor-based sample, the thesis follows a staged analytical strategy. The first step is to establish a robust exposure-vulnerability framework at municipality level. Flood exposure is derived consistently across several return periods, while socio-economic vulnerability is represented through a municipality-level indicator framework based on INKAR. This provides the empirical basis for the first research question. The second step concerns protection and modeled losses. Protection remains conceptually central to the thesis, but its empirical treatment requires greater caution than a simple binary distinction between protected and unprotected municipalities would allow. Research on residual risk and socio-hydrological feedbacks has shown repeatedly that protection can reduce frequent damage while still leaving substantial risk in place under more extreme conditions (Fu et al., 2023; Serra-Llobet et al., 2022; Tobin, 1995). For this reason, the thesis extends the exposure-vulnerability design toward protection-related interpretation through the provider loss/protection portfolio and the multi-return-period exposure curves.

Conceptually, the analysis links municipal flood exposure to municipal socio-economic vulnerability within one coherent study population. On the exposure side, the thesis works with a multi-return-period flood dataset at municipality level, including `RP10`, `RP20`, `RP50`, `RP100`, `RP200`, and `RP500`. This allows the analysis to move beyond one isolated hazard snapshot and to retain information about how exposure changes across return periods. For the main comparative analysis, `RP100` functions as the current benchmark, because it is both policy-relevant and analytically interpretable. On the vulnerability side, the thesis uses a municipality-level socio-economic index derived from INKAR. Protection is handled through modeled loss occurrence, finite protection return periods, and annual loss probabilities, while no-event municipalities are interpreted separately rather than forced into a binary protected-unprotected classification.

Study Area and Spatial Unit of Analysis
The thesis remains framed in terms of the Elbe river basin, because the basin provides the broader hydrological, historical, and governance context in which flood management and settlement development have evolved. Empirically, however, the analysis focuses on the flood-relevant Elbe corridor. A basin-wide inclusion of municipalities would also capture places that are hydrologically connected to the Elbe system but not substantively relevant for a comparison of flood exposure. de Moel et al. (2015) emphasize that flood-risk assessments differ substantially across spatial scales and study extents, while Sairam et al. (2021) show that spatial consistency matters strongly for the interpretation of flood patterns. The revised design therefore defines the study population through the extreme flood-relevant corridor rather than through the full basin alone. In practical terms, municipalities are retained if they intersect the `RP500` flood extent. This sharpens the analytical focus without abandoning the broader basin framing of the thesis.

The municipality forms the central unit of analysis in this thesis because it provides the most coherent scale at which the available flood and socio-economic data can be combined while still retaining meaningful spatial variation. This choice is not only practical, but also analytical. Flood-justice research has shown repeatedly that the relationship between social inequality and flood exposure is sensitive to spatial scale. de Moel et al. (2015) emphasize that flood-risk assessments differ markedly across scales and that both methodology and interpretation must be adjusted accordingly. Poussard et al. (2021) make the same point more directly in the context of environmental inequalities, showing that the apparent relationship between socio-economic status and flood exposure can change when the scale of analysis shifts from broader regions to smaller local units. For this reason, the municipality is used here as a middle scale that is local enough to reveal distributive spatial differences, but still broad enough to allow a consistent integration of flood rasters, administrative boundaries, and socio-economic indicators.

This scale also fits the German context particularly well. Municipalities are not only statistical units, but important governance units within flood risk management, spatial planning, and public service provision. In that sense, they are meaningful territorial entities through which spatial inequality can be described and later interpreted. German flood-risk research likewise often works with subnational or aggregated administrative units when linking physical hazard to social or economic information. Sairam et al. (2021), for example, stress the importance of spatial consistency in large-scale flood risk assessment for Germany, while Fekete (2009, 2010) demonstrates that social vulnerability to river floods can be operationalized meaningfully at subnational scale. The municipality therefore offers a defensible compromise between fine-scale sensitivity and analytical feasibility. It is not as detailed as a neighborhood or building-level study, but it is substantially more informative than broad county, regional, or basin-wide averages.

The empirical study population is defined through municipalities that intersect the `RP500` flood extent. This aligns the unit of analysis with the flood-relevant study corridor instead of with the entire Elbe basin. The basin remains the broader hydrological and governance frame of the thesis, but not every municipality in the basin is equally relevant for a justice-oriented comparison of flood exposure. By restricting the sample to municipalities that are actually part of the extreme flood corridor, the analysis becomes more coherent and more comparable across cases. At the same time, the municipality-level perspective makes it possible to connect the exposure analysis with the socio-economic structure of local territories. This does not mean that municipalities are internally homogeneous, nor that socio-economic groups are perfectly represented by municipal averages. It means, rather, that the thesis compares socially differentiated local contexts within one flood-relevant corridor and asks whether these contexts are associated with systematically different positions in the geography of flood exposure.

Equity Assessment Logic
The equity assessment of this thesis is primarily distributive. Its core question is whether flood-related burdens and protection/loss-related outcomes are patterned systematically across municipalities with different socio-economic profiles. This logic follows the flood-justice literature, which argues that fairness in flood risk management cannot be reduced to technical efficiency alone. Thaler and Hartmann (2016) show that flood risk management always involves decisions about who or what should be protected, while de Goër de Herve (2022) emphasizes that justice in this field includes both distributive and procedural dimensions. Building on these debates, the thesis focuses empirically on the distributive side. It asks whether municipalities that are more socially vulnerable also tend to face greater flood exposure, positive modeled loss occurrence, or weaker apparent protection-related outcomes. In that sense, equity is not treated as a moral slogan, but as an analytical question about the spatial alignment of social disadvantage and flood-related outcomes.

Operationally, this means comparing the geography of municipal vulnerability with the geography of municipal flood exposure. The first analytical step is therefore straightforward: a municipality-level socio-economic vulnerability profile is related to municipal flood-area shares, with `RP100` serving as the main benchmark and the broader multi-return-period structure retained for additional interpretation. This design does not assume that equal exposure across space is the only fair outcome, nor does it assume that every spatial difference is automatically unjust. As Johnson et al. (2007) argue, flooding is not fair in any simple natural sense, because hazard is inherently uneven across space. The justice-relevant issue begins when this physical unevenness overlaps systematically with social disadvantage or when public and private protection-related benefits are distributed in ways that reinforce rather than reduce existing inequalities. The empirical task of the thesis is therefore to identify whether such overlaps occur in the Elbe corridor and how strong they appear to be at municipality level.

This distributive logic also requires a cautious interpretation of what the analysis can show. The thesis does not identify the intentions of decision-makers, and it does not claim to isolate one single causal mechanism through which inequality and exposure become linked. Instead, it evaluates a justice-relevant spatial pattern. This is consistent with recent German and international work showing that differential flood exposure can be observed empirically at relatively local scales, even if the mechanisms behind it remain multiple and historically complex. Odersky and Löffler (2024) show that the 2021 floods in Germany displayed a regressive exposure pattern at the local level, while Tovar Reaños (2021) demonstrates that flood impacts and flood policy can interact with welfare and inequality in ways that disproportionately affect low-income households. For the present thesis, the implication is clear: if more vulnerable municipalities in the Elbe corridor are systematically more exposed, this is analytically meaningful as a distributive justice pattern, even if it does not by itself prove intentional discrimination or a single policy failure.

Scope, Assumptions & Limits
The design of this thesis is deliberately bounded, and these boundaries are important for interpreting its results correctly. First, the study is not a full-basin census of all municipalities that are hydrologically linked to the Elbe. It is a corridor-based analysis of municipalities intersecting the `RP500` flood extent. This improves flood relevance and comparability, but it also means that the results apply to the flood-relevant Elbe corridor rather than to the whole basin in an undifferentiated sense. Second, the municipality is an administrative and analytical unit, not a social group in itself. Municipal averages can reveal meaningful spatial differences, but they cannot capture within-municipal sorting, neighborhood-level heterogeneity, or household-level experiences. This scale issue is well known in both flood-risk and environmental-inequality research. The present study therefore makes a disciplined ecological claim: it compares socially differentiated municipalities, not individual households.

The exposure side of the analysis is also subject to clear scope conditions. The municipal flood indicators used here are area-based measures derived from modeled flood extents across several return periods. Their current primary role is to quantify how much of each municipality lies within the modeled flood footprint, especially for `RP100`, which functions as the main benchmark. This is a transparent and spatially consistent measure, but it is not identical to population exposure, building exposure, or monetary damage. A municipality may contain substantial flooded area while its settlement, population, or asset concentration is distributed unevenly within that area. More generally, large-scale flood assessments are shaped by simplifications in hazard modeling, exposure representation, and the treatment of return periods. Sairam et al. (2021) underline the importance of spatial consistency in German large-scale risk assessment, while the EFAS/JRC hazard-map literature and dataset documentation show that large-scale flood maps should be interpreted as modeled assessment products rather than as local legal hazard delineations (Alfieri et al., 2014; Baugh et al., 2024; Dottori et al., 2022). These issues do not invalidate area-based exposure analysis, but they define what the indicator can and cannot represent.

The vulnerability side requires similar caution. Social vulnerability indices are useful because they reduce complex, multidimensional social conditions to a structured and comparable spatial measure. At the same time, the literature is clear that such indices are sensitive to methodological choices. Schmidtlein et al. (2008) and Tate (2013) show that results can change substantially depending on indicator selection, normalization, weighting, aggregation, and scale. Rufat et al. (2019) likewise argue that most social vulnerability models are primarily descriptive and should not be treated as self-validating explanatory instruments. German flood-vulnerability research points in the same direction. Fekete (2009) explicitly notes that variable selection, thresholds, and aggregation involve assumptions, and that exact numerical weighting should be interpreted cautiously. This thesis responds to these concerns by treating the vulnerability index as an analytical tool rather than as a perfect measure of social reality. Its purpose is to reveal structured spatial patterns, not to assign immutable ranks to municipalities.

Finally, the treatment of protection in this thesis remains intentionally cautious. Protection is conceptually central, because the distribution of flood-related burdens cannot be understood fully without considering how hazard is modified, delayed, or reduced by protective arrangements. However, protection is not introduced as a simple binary municipality-level variable. Instead, the thesis uses the provider loss/protection portfolio to distinguish positive modeled loss municipalities with finite protection return-period estimates from municipalities with no simulated loss event, while also acknowledging the model limitation that smaller streams are not represented. This is a methodological decision, not a conceptual gap. Research on residual risk, levee effects, and socio-hydrological feedbacks shows clearly that protection is socially consequential but difficult to reduce to a single, spatially unambiguous indicator (Serra-Llobet et al., 2022; Fu et al., 2023). For that reason, the present design prefers transparency over premature precision. The study does not claim more than its data can support, and that restraint makes the analytical framework more defensible.

4. Data & Methods
This chapter explains how the research design developed in Chapter 3 is operationalized empirically. Its purpose is not to repeat the conceptual argument of the thesis, but to describe how flood exposure, socio-economic vulnerability, and protection-related exposure dynamics are translated into municipality-level data. The chapter therefore focuses on the active corridor-based workflow. Earlier processing routes, including the older BfG zone-based and direct protection-buffer approaches, are not presented as the main method because they have been superseded by the EFAS/JRC multi-return-period raster workflow. They remain relevant only as methodological background for why the current design is more cautious.

The workflow has five main components. First, administrative and hydrological spatial layers are harmonized so that municipal areas and flooded areas can be compared consistently. Second, multi-return-period flood-depth rasters are converted into municipality-level exposure indicators. Third, INKAR socio-economic indicators are processed, screened, and prepared for the construction of a vulnerability measure. Fourth, the multi-return-period exposure sequence is used to derive exposure-curve metrics that describe how modeled exposure accumulates across return periods. Fifth, a simulation-based damage/loss and protection return-period table is joined to the corridor municipalities and treated as a separate protection module. This structure follows the scale-sensitive logic of flood-risk assessment: data sources, spatial units, model assumptions, and interpretation are closely connected (de Moel et al., 2015; Poussard et al., 2021).

4.1 Data Sources
The spatial unit of analysis is the municipality. Municipality geometries are taken from the VG250 administrative boundary dataset of the Bundesamt fuer Kartographie und Geodaesie (BKG). VG250 is used because it provides a consistent national layer of German administrative units and can be linked to socio-economic data through official municipality identifiers. In this thesis, the municipal polygons serve three roles at once: they define the unit of comparison, provide the area denominator for flood shares, and form the spatial join framework through which flood exposure and INKAR indicators are combined.

The broader hydrological context is represented by an Elbe river basin polygon from the Bundesanstalt fuer Gewaesserkunde (BfG). The basin layer is not used to define the final empirical population of municipalities. Instead, it provides the contextual frame for the Elbe case and serves as a processing mask for the flood rasters. This distinction is central to the revised design of the thesis. The thesis remains conceptually framed around the Elbe basin, but the empirical sample is the flood-relevant Elbe corridor, defined through the RP500 flood extent.

Flood hazard information is taken from European multi-return-period river flood hazard rasters associated with the Copernicus Emergency Management Service and the Joint Research Centre. The relevant dataset family is the JRC *River flood hazard maps for Europe and the Mediterranean Basin region* dataset (Baugh et al., 2024), with methodological links to European flood hazard modeling work by Alfieri et al. (2014) and Dottori et al. (2022). The rasters represent modeled flood water depth for different return periods and are intended for exposure and flood-risk assessment. They are not official German flood hazard maps and are therefore not interpreted as legal flood-zone delineations. In this thesis, they are used as a spatially consistent modeled hazard source that allows exposure to be compared across municipalities within the same analytical framework.

The analysis uses six return periods: `RP10`, `RP20`, `RP50`, `RP100`, `RP200`, and `RP500`. `RP500` is used to define the empirical corridor because it identifies the broad flood-relevant municipality population under an extreme modeled event. `RP100` serves as the current main benchmark for comparative exposure analysis because it is widely interpretable in flood-risk work and provides a meaningful reference point between frequent and rare events. All return periods are retained, however, because they allow exposure to be represented as a sequence rather than as a single hazard snapshot.

Socio-economic data are taken from INKAR, the interactive online atlas of the Federal Institute for Research on Building, Urban Affairs and Spatial Development (BBSR). The working dataset is the INKAR 07/2025 edition, with data prepared on a consistent territorial status as of 31.12.2023. INKAR provides municipality-level indicators on demographic structure, socio-economic conditions, accessibility, labor-market conditions, housing, infrastructure, and other local characteristics. In this thesis, INKAR is not treated as a ready-made vulnerability dataset. Instead, the available indicators are reviewed, screened, and reduced to a final vulnerability-index input set, while PCA is retained as a diagnostic and robustness tool. This reflects the context-dependent character of social vulnerability indicators and the need to interpret them through both conceptual relevance and data structure (Rufat et al., 2015; Rufat et al., 2019).

Protection-related information is introduced through a municipality-level damage/loss and protection return-period table, `elbe_protection_level_mun.csv`. The table contains municipality identifiers, municipality names, the number of non-zero loss years in the simulation output, annual loss probabilities, and derived protection return periods. Because the table is derived from modeled losses, it is not a complete protection-infrastructure inventory and it does not provide a direct binary distinction between protected and unprotected municipalities. For the title's focus on flood protection infrastructure, the table is therefore used as modeled evidence of protection-related outcomes, not as a register of individual protection assets. It identifies municipalities for which modeled flood losses occur in the provider's flood portfolio and for which a finite protection return period can therefore be estimated. Following provider clarification, municipalities absent from this table are interpreted as municipalities with no simulated loss event and modeled annual loss probability zero in the provider flood portfolio, not as municipalities without real-world flood risk or without flood protection. For larger cities such as Hamburg and Magdeburg, this is interpreted as a consequence of high protection levels in the simulation. For smaller streams, the provider simulation does not provide complete representation; this is treated as a limitation. The simulation characteristics are interpreted with reference to Sairam et al. (2021), who describe the Regional Flood Model as a process-based, spatially consistent flood risk model for Germany based on a 5000-year event catalogue, coupled hydrological and hydrodynamic modeling, and sector-specific exposure and loss models.

The German Index of Socioeconomic Deprivation (GISD) was prepared as a possible contextual or validation dataset, but it is not part of the main active workflow at this stage. The same applies to earlier BfG flood-zone and protection-layer approaches. Those sources remain useful background and may support later interpretation, but the core exposure workflow in this thesis is based on the EFAS/JRC multi-return-period raster data.

4.2 Spatial Preprocessing and Study Area Definition
All spatial data used in the active workflow are harmonized to `EPSG:25832` (ETRS89 / UTM Zone 32N). This coordinate reference system is used because the core exposure metrics are area-based. Municipal flooded area and total municipality area must be calculated in a projected coordinate system with meter units; otherwise, the resulting flood shares would be difficult to interpret and potentially inconsistent across space. CRS harmonization is therefore not only a technical step, but a prerequisite for producing comparable exposure indicators.

The municipality layer required an additional cleaning step. In the VG250-derived workflow, municipalities can appear with different `Geofaktor_GF` attributes. For the purpose of flood-share calculation, only polygons with `Geofaktor_GF = "mit Struktur Land"` are used. This land-structure filter avoids using water-only or otherwise unsuitable geometry variants as the denominator for municipal area. Without this step, flood shares can be distorted because the area denominator may no longer represent the municipality in the intended administrative sense. The filter is therefore retained as a core methodological decision.

The flood rasters were first clipped to the Elbe basin frame to reduce processing volume and align the hazard inputs with the case-study context. The analytical sample was then defined more narrowly. Municipalities are included in the final corridor if they have non-zero flooded area in the `RP500` flood raster. In practical terms, the workflow first identifies municipalities intersecting valid `RP500` flood-depth pixels and then performs an exact flooded-area extraction for this preselected set. Municipalities with positive `RP500` flooded area are retained.

The resulting analytical sample consists of 835 corridor municipalities. The Elbe basin is therefore the broader hydrological and governance frame, while the empirical population is the `RP500` flood corridor. This distinction prevents the analysis from mixing flood-relevant municipalities with large numbers of basin municipalities that have no modeled flood exposure in the selected raster data. It also makes the exposure-vulnerability comparison more defensible, because all municipalities in the sample belong to the same broad flood-relevant study population. The choice is consistent with the wider literature showing that flood-risk and flood-inequality results depend strongly on spatial scale and study extent (de Moel et al., 2015; Poussard et al., 2021).

4.3 Flood Hazard and Exposure Construction
Flood exposure is derived from the prepared flood-depth rasters. The rasters are interpreted as water-depth grids in which valid depth cells represent flooded areas and `NoData` cells represent non-flooded areas. The main workflow does not require raster polygonization. Instead, it uses raster-based zonal extraction to calculate the area of valid flood-depth cells intersecting each municipality. This approach follows the general logic of exposure assessment, in which hazard footprints are spatially overlaid with exposed units of analysis (de Moel et al., 2015; Qiang, 2019).

For each return period, a cell-area raster is created and masked by the corresponding flood-depth raster. The masked cell areas are then summed within each municipality. This produces a flooded area value in square meters for each municipality and return period. Flood share is calculated as:

```text
flood_share_rp = flood_area_rp_m2 / municipality_area_m2
```

The resulting wide exposure table contains municipality area, flooded area, and flood share for `RP10`, `RP20`, `RP50`, `RP100`, `RP200`, and `RP500`. The main empirical comparison uses `flood_share_rp100`, but the multi-return-period structure is retained throughout the workflow. This allows the analysis to describe not only how much of a municipality is exposed under one benchmark event, but also how exposure accumulates as return periods become rarer. This is useful because return-period choice affects the interpretation of flood-risk assessments and because multi-return-period data can reveal patterns that a single hazard layer would hide (Rojas et al., 2013; de Moel et al., 2015).

The main exposure metric is area-based rather than depth-weighted. This is a deliberate choice. Area-based flood share is transparent, stable across all return periods, and directly comparable across municipalities of different sizes. It is also well suited to the distributive comparison used in this thesis, because it captures the spatial overlap between modeled flood hazard and municipal territory. At the same time, the limitation is clear: this indicator does not distinguish shallow inundation from deep inundation, and it does not measure population exposure, asset exposure, or monetary damage. A municipality with a high flood share is therefore not automatically a municipality with high expected damage. It is a municipality with a larger share of its territory inside the modeled flood footprint.

This interpretation is consistent with the broader flood-risk literature, where exposure indicators depend strongly on the scale, unit, and purpose of the assessment (de Moel et al., 2015; Sairam et al., 2021). The exposure indicator used here is designed for a spatial distributive comparison, not for detailed hydraulic damage modeling. Depth-weighted, population-weighted, or settlement-weighted exposure metrics remain possible extensions, but they are not the primary metric in the current thesis design.

4.4 Exposure Quality Checks
The exposure pipeline includes automated quality checks because the analysis combines large raster layers with administrative geometries and area calculations. Three types of checks are applied. First, flood shares are checked to ensure that they fall within the valid range between 0 and 1. Second, flooded areas are checked to ensure that they do not exceed total municipality area. Third, monotonicity across return periods is checked. In principle, modeled flood extent should not decrease as return periods become rarer, so the expected order is:

```text
RP10 <= RP20 <= RP50 <= RP100 <= RP200 <= RP500
```

Municipalities that violate any of these checks are exported for inspection. This does not automatically mean that the affected municipalities are unusable, because small non-monotonicities can arise from raster processing, reprojection, resampling, or model artifacts. However, the flags make such cases visible and prevent the analysis from treating the exposure table as unproblematic by default.

In the current output, all flood shares fall within valid bounds and no flooded area exceeds total municipal area. The only remaining issue concerns two small monotonicity deviations: `13076114` Plau am See, Stadt and `16077005` Fockendorf. Both deviations are very small and are treated as processing artifacts rather than substantive exposure reversals. For the exposure-curve metrics described below, observed non-monotonic cases are adjusted using a simple cumulative maximum transformation. This preserves the observed exposure sequence as far as possible while enforcing the logical condition that exposure should not decline with rarer return periods. The raw exposure values remain available in the underlying exposure table, while the adjusted values are used only for derived curve metrics that require monotonicity.

4.5 Socio-Economic Data Processing
The socio-economic workflow starts from the full INKAR long-format data file. Because the raw file is large, the import is handled with efficient tabular processing tools. The data are filtered to municipality-level records and prepared so that the latest available observation for each municipality and indicator is retained. Not all INKAR indicators are available for the same reference year. Rather than imposing one universal year that would drop many indicators, the workflow uses the latest available value per municipality-indicator combination and documents the resulting coverage.

After filtering and latest-value selection, the data are transformed from long to wide format. The municipality identifier `AGS` is used as the join key between INKAR and the corridor municipality layer. The final socio-economic table is therefore aligned with the same municipal units used in the exposure workflow. AGS-prefix filtering is treated only as a processing step, not as the definition of the study area. The study area is defined spatially by the `RP500` corridor; INKAR is then joined to this spatially defined sample.

The corridor contains 835 municipalities. In the current latest-build INKAR table, 834 of these municipalities are matched with socio-economic records. One municipality, `16076094` Berga-Wünschendorf, remains missing in the current raw INKAR build and is documented explicitly. As a result, vulnerability-index calculations that depend on INKAR are carried out for the municipalities with available INKAR records, while the exposure dataset itself remains available for all 835 corridor municipalities.

Within the corridor, 176 original INKAR indicators are available in the latest-build table. These indicators form the full diagnostic inventory. They are not all used directly in the final vulnerability index, because many indicators are redundant, conceptually ambiguous, weakly connected to vulnerability, or unsuitable because of coverage problems. The next step is therefore a substantive and statistical screening of the available indicator pool. This step is consistent with the vulnerability literature, which treats indicator selection and aggregation as central methodological choices rather than as purely technical details (Fekete, 2009; Rufat et al., 2015; Tate, 2013).

4.6 Indicator Screening and Candidate Vulnerability Variables
The indicator-screening stage responds to a central challenge in social vulnerability measurement: vulnerability indices are sensitive to the variables included, the scale of analysis, the handling of missingness, and the aggregation method (Schmidtlein et al., 2008; Tate, 2013; Rufat et al., 2015; Rufat et al., 2019). For this reason, the thesis does not move directly from all available INKAR indicators to a final index. Instead, the 176 available indicators are first reviewed and grouped into broad substantive dimensions, including socio-economic status, labor-market position, demographic structure, household structure, accessibility and services, digital infrastructure, housing and land-use context, and public finance or local context variables.

Coverage rates are calculated for all indicators. Indicators with poor coverage are flagged because they would reduce the number of usable municipalities or require extensive imputation. A correlation screening is then used diagnostically to identify very similar or nested variables. Inductive techniques such as PCA can overweight a latent construct if several near-duplicate indicators are included. Correlation screening is therefore not used mechanically to remove variables, but it helps identify where conceptual judgment is needed.

Several specific decisions follow from this review. The indicators `a_ALGII_SGBII` and `a_Unterkunft_SGBII` both capture closely related SGB-II deprivation patterns and are not treated as independent core deprivation indicators in the same main index. In the current corridor data, the pair is based on 825 complete observations and has a correlation of approximately `-0.70`, which indicates strong overlap but not a perfect mathematical complement. The indicator `a_BG1P` is not interpreted as single-parent households, because its official meaning refers to one-person benefit communities rather than a clean family-structure category. Student indicators are treated cautiously because they may reflect socio-economic precariousness, but they also strongly capture university-town structure. For that reason, student variables are not included in the main vulnerability specification and are retained only as a sensitivity block. Population density and employment density are also kept outside the main index because they describe settlement context rather than vulnerability in themselves.

The screening process produces several diagnostic input sets. The full `all_176` set is retained as a complete indicator inventory, not as a final index. The earlier `original_51` set remains useful as an exploratory benchmark because it reflects the first broad PCA design, but it contains duplicated and definition-sensitive constructs. The `student_sensitivity_26` set adds student-related indicators to test how much university-town structure affects the vulnerability pattern. A stricter 17-variable curated set is available as an additional review option. The final main specification uses the intermediate `thesis_candidate_23` set because it offers the best balance between breadth and interpretability: it is substantially cleaner than the original 51-variable block, but still broad enough to represent demographic sensitivity, household structure, economic deprivation, labor-market conditions, accessibility, health-care access, and digital connectivity.

The 23 indicators are organized into three dimensions. The first dimension, demographic and household structure, includes the share of residents aged 65 and older, households with children, single-person households, residents under six years, and single-parent employable SGB-II recipients. The second dimension, deprivation and labor-market position, includes SGB-II deprivation, low-income households, child poverty, long-term unemployment, unemployment among younger and older workers, marginal employment, and reverse-coded indicators of income tax, purchasing power, and employment rate. The third dimension, access and adaptive capacity, includes distances to supermarkets, public transport, pharmacies, general practitioners, and primary schools, as well as reverse-coded indicators of physician supply, 4G availability, and fixed broadband availability. The resulting variable set is documented in the exported file `corridor_vulnerability_index_variables.csv`.

4.7 Vulnerability Index Construction and PCA Diagnostics
The final vulnerability index is constructed as a direction-coded and dimension-balanced composite rather than as a mechanically weighted PCA score. This choice follows from both the literature and the empirical structure of the corridor data. German river-flood vulnerability work has used factor-analytic approaches to identify dimensions of social vulnerability, but also emphasizes that aggregation and validation require substantive interpretation (Fekete, 2009). More broadly, social vulnerability indices are known to be sensitive to indicator choice, scale, weighting, and aggregation (Schmidtlein et al., 2008; Tate, 2013; Rufat et al., 2015). Rufat et al. (2019) further caution that vulnerability models should not be treated as self-validating simply because they produce an internally consistent score. In this thesis, PCA is therefore used to inspect the structure and robustness of the selected indicators, while the main index is built to preserve the conceptual balance between the three vulnerability dimensions.

The index is calculated for the 834 corridor municipalities with available INKAR records. The one corridor municipality without a current INKAR match, `16076094` Berga-Wünschendorf, remains in the exposure dataset but receives no vulnerability-index value. For municipalities with INKAR records, remaining indicator-level missing values are imputed with the median of the respective variable. All 23 variables are then transformed to z-scores. Indicators for which higher values indicate greater capacity rather than vulnerability, such as purchasing power, income tax per capita, employment rate, physician supply, and broadband availability, are multiplied by `-1` after standardization. After this direction coding, higher values consistently indicate higher expected social vulnerability or lower adaptive capacity.

For each municipality `i`, the dimension score for dimension `d` is calculated as the mean of the direction-coded standardized variables belonging to that dimension and then standardized again:

```text
D_id = z(mean(z*_ij))
```

where `z*_ij` is the direction-coded z-score of indicator `j`. The final vulnerability index is the standardized mean of the three dimension scores:

```text
V_i = z(mean(D_i1, D_i2, D_i3))
```

This procedure gives equal weight to the three conceptual dimensions rather than to every individual indicator. Several variables in the accessibility and service-provision dimension are highly correlated, so a purely variable-level average or a purely inductive PCA score would allow the strongest covariance structure to dominate the index. The dimension-balanced approach instead keeps demographic/household sensitivity, deprivation/labor-market position, and access/adaptive capacity analytically visible while still producing one municipality-level vulnerability score for the distributive comparison with flood exposure and protection-related outcomes.

PCA is retained as a diagnostic and robustness tool. A PCA is run on the same direction-coded 23-variable input matrix using `prcomp()`. Component scores, eigenvalues, explained variance, cumulative variance, and loadings are exported. Eight components are retained in the main PCA sensitivity score because they exceed the 70 percent cumulative-variance threshold, explaining 72.60 percent of the variance. Six components satisfy the Kaiser criterion of eigenvalues above one and explain 64.45 percent of the variance. The two PCA sensitivity scores are almost identical (`r = 0.997`), which indicates that the PCA result is stable with respect to this component-retention choice. However, the dimension-balanced main index and the 8-component PCA sensitivity score are only moderately correlated (`r = 0.441`; Spearman `rho = 0.473`). This difference is substantively informative rather than a technical error: the PCA gives strong weight to the dominant spatial covariance structure in the corridor, especially accessibility and settlement-periphery patterns, whereas the main index deliberately balances accessibility with deprivation and demographic/household vulnerability.

The vulnerability index is therefore interpreted as a descriptive municipality-level measure of socio-economic vulnerability, not as a causal estimate and not as a direct measure of individual household vulnerability. Flood protection variables are not included in the vulnerability index. Protection return periods, annual loss probabilities, and no-event municipalities are analyzed separately as flood-risk and residual-risk outcomes. This separation avoids mixing social susceptibility and adaptive capacity with the flood-protection outcome that the thesis seeks to compare against social vulnerability.

4.8 Multi-Return-Period Exposure Curves and Protection-Related Interpretation
The multi-return-period exposure table is also used to construct municipality-level exposure curves. Each municipality has a sequence of flood shares across `RP10`, `RP20`, `RP50`, `RP100`, `RP200`, and `RP500`. These curves describe how much of the final `RP500` exposure is already realized at more frequent return periods and how much is added only under rarer events. This preserves information that would be lost if the analysis relied only on `RP100`.

Several metrics are derived from these curves. `onset_rp_any` identifies the first return period with any positive flood share. `onset_rp_1pct` and `onset_rp_5pct` identify the first return period at which at least one or five percent of municipal area is flooded. `slope_logrp` measures the increase in flood share over the log-transformed return period scale. `normalized_auc` summarizes the area under the normalized exposure curve; higher values indicate that final exposure is realized earlier. `realized_by_rp100` measures the share of final RP500 exposure already present by RP100, while `late_growth_share` measures the share added after RP100. Finally, `max_jump_interval` and `max_jump_rel` identify where the largest increase occurs along the return-period sequence.

These metrics support a first descriptive typology of exposure dynamics. Municipalities can be classified as `early exposure`, `gradual increase`, or `delayed jump`. Early-exposure municipalities already realize much of their final `RP500` exposure by `RP100` or earlier. Delayed-jump municipalities show a larger increase only after `RP100`, especially between `RP100` and `RP200` or between `RP200` and `RP500`. Gradual-increase municipalities fall between these patterns. In the current curve output, most corridor municipalities fall into the early-exposure category, while smaller groups show delayed-jump or gradual-increase dynamics.

The methodological value of these curves lies in their relationship to protection and residual risk. Protection should not be treated as a simple binary condition, because protected areas can still face residual risk, overtopping, failure, or exposure under rarer events (Tobin, 1995; Serra-Llobet et al., 2022; Fu et al., 2023). The exposure curves do not directly measure protection infrastructure, and they do not prove why exposure appears early or late. However, they provide a structured way to describe how modeled exposure accumulates across return periods. This remains useful even after the integration of the damage/loss-based protection table, because the two sources describe different things: the exposure curves describe the spatial accumulation of modeled flooded area, while the protection table describes modeled loss occurrence and derived protection return periods within the provider flood portfolio.

For this reason, the thesis treats exposure-curve metrics as complementary to, rather than interchangeable with, the protection return-period data. They can help identify municipalities where exposure is already present under frequent events and municipalities where exposure increases mainly under rarer events. Both patterns are relevant for interpreting residual risk, but neither should be overread as direct evidence of the presence, absence, or performance of specific flood protection infrastructure. This cautious interpretation is also consistent with the wider residual-risk literature, which emphasizes that maps and protection standards can represent areas behind protection very differently (Serra-Llobet et al., 2022).

In the Results chapter, these exposure-curve metrics are therefore used as a complementary descriptive layer. They are not merged mechanically into the protection return-period variable, but they help interpret whether municipalities with similar `RP100` exposure differ in how exposure accumulates across more frequent and rarer return periods.

4.9 Damage-Loss Portfolio and Protection Return-Period Data
The protection data are incorporated after the exposure and socio-economic datasets have been constructed. The file `elbe_protection_level_mun.csv` is joined to the RP500 corridor municipalities through the official municipality identifier `AGS`. This join is deliberately carried out as a separate module rather than as part of the corridor definition. The empirical corridor remains defined by modeled EFAS/JRC `RP500` flood exposure, while the protection table represents the subset of municipalities for which the provider flood portfolio produces positive modeled losses and an associated finite protection return-period estimate.

The table contains `301` municipalities in total. Of these, `280` match the `835` municipalities in the RP500 corridor. The remaining `555` corridor municipalities are absent from the table. Written provider clarification is central for interpreting this result (N. Sairam and T. N. M. Phan, personal communication, July 2026): no municipalities were removed from the table because of a minimum event threshold, and there is no 30-event filter in the current version. Instead, the table is derived directly from the modeled loss data. Municipalities absent from the table are therefore coded as municipalities with no simulated loss event and modeled annual loss probability zero in the provider flood portfolio.

This coding distinction is central to the protection analysis. A municipality without a protection return-period value is not interpreted as unprotected, risk-free, or missing at random. It is interpreted as a no-event case in the provider simulation. For large cities such as Hamburg or Magdeburg, the provider interpretation is that the protection level is high enough that no loss event appears in the simulated event catalogue. For smaller streams, however, the provider clarification also indicates a model limitation, because smaller streams are not considered. This limitation is interpreted in relation to the broader process-based flood-risk modeling framework described by Sairam et al. (2021). It creates a difference between the thesis exposure corridor and the provider loss/protection output. The full corridor remains the relevant population for the exposure-vulnerability analysis, because all `835` municipalities have modeled EFAS/JRC flood exposure under `RP500`. By contrast, finite protection-return-period analysis is restricted to the `280` matched municipalities with modeled losses and estimated protection return periods, while the remaining corridor municipalities can be treated as zero modeled annual loss probability for probability-based summaries.

Two diagnostic checks are used to document the no-event pattern. First, a protection coverage map classifies corridor municipalities as either `Finite protection RP available` or `No simulated loss event`. Larger cities and Elbe-near municipalities are labelled to make visible whether no-event cases affect only small peripheral municipalities or also larger settlements. Second, a stream-context check compares no-event municipalities with the Elbe main-stem layer and WFD river-water-body data. This diagnostic assesses whether municipalities without simulated loss events are mainly associated with smaller rivers or water bodies. The result supports this explanation only partly: many no-event municipalities intersect only minor or other WFD water bodies, but a non-negligible group is located directly on or near the Elbe, including larger cities for which the provider interpretation points to high modeled protection levels.

The methodological consequence is that the protection module has two related but distinct parts. First, finite protection return periods are compared only for the positive-loss subset. Second, the absence of simulated losses is retained as meaningful information and can be interpreted as modeled annual loss probability zero, while acknowledging the smaller-stream limitation. The thesis can therefore compare protection return periods, annual loss probabilities, exposure, and vulnerability for the matched municipalities, while also reporting the spatial pattern of no-event municipalities. It does not assign an arbitrary finite protection return period to the no-event municipalities and does not infer from their absence that they have no flood protection or no real-world flood risk.

In the Results chapter, no-event municipalities are displayed explicitly as `No simulated loss event` and, where annual probability is summarized, as modeled annual loss probability `0`. They are not assigned artificial finite protection return periods.

4.10 Statistical and Spatial Analysis
The empirical analysis proceeds in stages. First, descriptive statistics summarize the flood corridor, the distribution of flood shares across return periods, and the distribution of socio-economic vulnerability. Maps are used to show the `RP500` corridor, `RP100` exposure, and the spatial pattern of the vulnerability index. These maps are descriptive tools and help identify broad spatial patterns before statistical comparison.

Second, the main distributive comparison relates municipality-level vulnerability to flood exposure. The benchmark outcome is `flood_share_rp100`. Bivariate associations are used to assess whether more vulnerable municipalities tend to have higher or lower `RP100` flood shares. The analysis combines Pearson and Spearman correlations with grouped comparisons across vulnerability quintiles and a bivariate tercile map of exposure and vulnerability. This is consistent with the distributive logic of the thesis: the main question is whether flood exposure aligns systematically with socio-economic vulnerability. Comparable exposure-disparity studies use this kind of spatial overlay and group comparison logic to connect flood hazard or exposure with social differentiation (Qiang, 2019; Poussard et al., 2021; Odersky and Löffler, 2024).

Third, exposure-curve metrics are used to extend the analysis beyond `RP100`. Metrics such as `realized_by_rp100`, `late_growth_share`, and `max_jump_interval` allow the thesis to ask whether municipalities with different vulnerability profiles also differ in how exposure accumulates across return periods. The analysis therefore compares vulnerability not only with one exposure benchmark, but also with the shape of the exposure curve.

Fourth, the damage/loss-based protection data are analyzed in two ways. For the `280` municipalities with positive modeled losses, finite protection return periods and annual loss probabilities are mapped and summarized, and their association with socio-economic vulnerability is explored descriptively. For the remaining corridor municipalities, the provider interpretation is not a missing value but a no-event result: their modeled annual loss probability is treated as zero. These municipalities are therefore retained in the full exposure-vulnerability analysis and separately reported as `no simulated loss event / zero modeled annual loss probability`. This prevents the protection analysis from mixing finite return-period estimates with no-event cases while still preserving the substantive information contained in the absence of simulated losses.

The statistical analysis is therefore deliberately descriptive and distributive. It uses maps, correlations, vulnerability quantile comparisons, bivariate exposure-vulnerability classifications, and protection/loss summaries. The thesis does not rely on a causal regression model as the primary evidentiary device, because the available data are better suited to identifying spatial-distributive patterns than to reconstructing the causal history of municipal flood protection.

The workflow is implemented primarily in R. Vector spatial processing is carried out with `sf`, raster processing and zonal extraction with `terra`, large tabular imports with `data.table`, data wrangling with `dplyr` and `tidyr`, and mapping with `ggplot2` and `geom_sf()`. QGIS is used for visual inspection and selected base-layer preparation, but the core analytical outputs are generated through scripted workflows.

The workflow separates flood-raster preparation, municipality-level exposure extraction, socio-economic data processing, vulnerability-index construction, PCA diagnostics, exposure-curve analysis, protection-data integration, and statistical comparison. Outputs are written to organized folders as CSV tables, GeoPackage layers, maps, plots, logs, and diagnostic files. This structure supports reproducibility because it allows the exposure data, INKAR preparation, PCA diagnostics, protection coverage checks, and final analysis table to be inspected separately.

The main exposure outputs include the `RP500` corridor layer, return-period-specific exposure tables, the wide all-return-period exposure table, and exposure quality-check files. The main corridor-analysis outputs include the `RP100` analysis table, the corridor vulnerability map, the `RP100` exposure map, and the corridor GeoPackage with exposure and PCA outputs. The exposure-curve workflow adds curve metrics, long-format curve tables, typology summaries, and maps of curve types. The protection workflow adds a joined corridor protection GeoPackage, coverage and return-period maps, positive-loss and no-event municipality tables, a large-municipality coverage table, and a stream-context diagnostic for municipalities without simulated loss events in the provider output. Together, these outputs provide the empirical basis for the Results chapter.

5.7 Results Synthesis
The results answer the two research questions in different ways. For the first research question, the analysis does not find a simple positive relationship between socio-economic vulnerability and RP100 municipal area exposure. The RP100 exposure-vulnerability correlation is close to zero, and the quintile comparison is non-monotonic. The lowest-vulnerability quintile has the highest mean RP100 flood share, while the highest-vulnerability quintile still contains many strongly exposed municipalities but does not form a clear corridor-wide exposure maximum. The bivariate map confirms this: high-exposure and high-vulnerability municipalities exist, but high exposure also occurs among low- and medium-vulnerability municipalities. The exposure result is therefore spatially heterogeneous rather than straightforwardly regressive.

The multi-return-period analysis strengthens this interpretation. Most corridor municipalities are classified as early-exposure municipalities, and the mean exposure curves by vulnerability quintile remain broadly consistent across return periods. The RP100 benchmark therefore does not appear to hide a fundamentally different vulnerability pattern at rarer events. It captures a corridor in which much of the eventual RP500 area exposure is already present by RP100 or earlier. The main exposure finding is thus not that vulnerable municipalities are uniformly more exposed, but that flood-area exposure is widespread, uneven, and only weakly associated with the final vulnerability index at municipality level.

The second research question receives a different answer. The provider protection/loss portfolio shows a clearer social pattern than the RP100 area-exposure comparison. Positive modeled losses and finite protection return-period estimates are more common in the higher vulnerability groups than in the lowest-vulnerability group. When no-event municipalities are retained as zero modeled annual loss probability, mean annual loss probability rises from `0.9%` in the lowest-vulnerability quintile to `4.8%` in Q4 and `3.3%` in the highest-vulnerability quintile. Among positive-loss municipalities only, the median finite return period falls from `156` years in the lowest-vulnerability quintile to `28` years in the highest-vulnerability quintile, with some variation in the middle groups. The loss/protection layer therefore contains a clearer social differentiation than the area-exposure layer alone.

At the same time, this result depends on the interpretation of the no-event class. The provider clarification described in Section 4.9 makes it possible to treat absent municipalities as zero modeled annual loss probability, not as ordinary missing values. However, the same clarification also requires caution: smaller streams are not fully represented, and no-event outcomes may reflect high modeled protection in some larger cities while reflecting model scope in other municipalities. The empirical conclusion is therefore not that no-event municipalities have no real flood risk. It is that they have no simulated loss event in the provider portfolio used here.

Taken together, the Results chapter shows that the flood-justice question cannot be answered by area exposure alone. RP100 flood shares reveal a heterogeneous and weakly socially patterned exposure geography. The protection/loss portfolio adds a second layer in which positive modeled losses, annual loss probabilities, and finite protection return periods show a clearer association with socio-economic vulnerability. This difference between exposure and modeled loss/protection outcomes is the central empirical bridge into the Discussion chapter. It raises the question of how flood justice should be interpreted when physical flood-area exposure is not strongly regressive, but modeled loss and protection-related outcomes appear more socially differentiated.

6. Discussion
The results of this thesis complicate a simple distributive flood-justice expectation. The Elbe corridor does not show a clear pattern in which municipalities with higher socio-economic vulnerability also have systematically higher RP100 municipal area exposure. The exposure-vulnerability correlation is close to zero, the quintile pattern is non-monotonic, and high exposure occurs across the vulnerability distribution. At the same time, this does not mean that the justice question disappears. Once the provider protection/loss portfolio is added, the empirical pattern becomes more socially differentiated. Positive modeled losses are more common in the upper vulnerability groups than in the lowest-vulnerability group, modeled annual loss probability is weakly but positively associated with vulnerability, and finite protection return periods tend to be lower among more vulnerable municipalities within the positive-loss subset.

The discussion therefore starts from a distinction that is central to the thesis as a whole: flood-area exposure, socio-economic vulnerability, and modeled loss/protection outcomes are related, but they are not the same thing. Flood justice cannot be assessed adequately from a single spatial overlay alone. The literature on flood inequalities repeatedly shows that the relationship between social disadvantage and flood exposure depends on scale, metric, and context (Poussard et al., 2021; Qiang, 2019). At the same time, flood justice is not only about where water can go, but also about how protection, residual risk, and recovery capacity distribute the consequences of flooding across social groups and places (de Goër de Herve, 2022; Thaler and Hartmann, 2016). The contribution of this thesis lies in making these layers visible separately for the Elbe corridor.

6.1 Interpreting the Main Empirical Pattern
The main empirical finding is not a simple null result. It would be misleading to conclude that flood risk in the Elbe corridor is socially neutral simply because RP100 municipal area exposure is only weakly associated with the final vulnerability index. The results show something more specific: the spatial distribution of modeled flooded area does not align strongly with socio-economic vulnerability at the municipality scale, but modeled loss occurrence and protection-related outcomes show a clearer social differentiation. The thesis therefore shifts the interpretation from a one-dimensional exposure question to a multi-dimensional risk question.

This multi-dimensional interpretation fits the conceptual framing developed in Chapter 2. Flood justice is concerned with the distribution of flood-related burdens and benefits, but those burdens and benefits are not exhausted by flood extent alone. A municipality may have a high flood share because large parts of its administrative territory lie in the floodplain, while the settlement structure, assets, or population may be arranged in ways that reduce modeled losses. Conversely, a municipality with a lower area-based flood share may still contain vulnerable settlements or exposed assets that produce positive modeled losses. This is why the protection/loss portfolio changes the empirical picture. It adds an outcome layer that is closer to modeled damage occurrence than the EFAS/JRC area-exposure indicator.

The results therefore refine rather than contradict the flood-justice literature. Studies such as Qiang (2019), Poussard et al. (2021), and Odersky and Löffler (2024) show that flood exposure can be socially unequal, but they also demonstrate that scale and spatial framing matter. The present thesis finds no strong regressive pattern in municipal RP100 area exposure, but it does find that vulnerability becomes more relevant when the analysis moves toward modeled losses and apparent protection-related outcomes. The Elbe corridor therefore does not support a simple statement that more vulnerable municipalities are generally more exposed in terms of flooded municipal area. It supports a more specific statement: the social distribution of flood-related disadvantage becomes more visible when exposure is interpreted together with modeled loss occurrence and protection-related residual risk.

6.2 Why RP100 Area Exposure Does Not Show a Simple Social Gradient
The weak relationship between RP100 exposure and socio-economic vulnerability needs to be interpreted in light of the exposure metric used in this thesis. The main exposure variable measures the share of municipal area covered by modeled flood depth cells. This is a transparent and reproducible spatial indicator, but it is not equivalent to population exposure, building exposure, asset exposure, depth-weighted exposure, or monetary damage. This distinction matters especially in a municipality-level design. Municipalities differ substantially in size, settlement pattern, land use, and the location of built-up areas within the administrative boundary. A high municipal flood share can therefore reflect extensive floodplain territory without necessarily implying equally high population or asset exposure.

This helps explain why the lowest-vulnerability quintile can have the highest mean RP100 flood share without implying that socially advantaged populations necessarily carry the highest flood burden. Some less vulnerable municipalities may include large areas of floodplain, agricultural land, or low-density territory. At the same time, some more vulnerable municipalities may have lower total municipal flood shares but still contain socially sensitive populations or assets in flood-relevant locations. The area-based exposure measure is well suited for comparing the spatial extent of flood hazard across municipalities, but it cannot by itself describe who within the municipality is exposed or how severe the consequences would be.

The municipality scale also smooths internal spatial inequalities. This is a necessary compromise in the present thesis because the available exposure, INKAR, and protection/loss data can be integrated consistently at municipal level. However, it means that within-municipality variation remains invisible. Flood-inequality patterns that appear clearly at neighborhood, block, or household scale may be weakened when aggregated to municipal boundaries. This is consistent with the broader methodological literature. de Moel et al. (2015) show that flood-risk assessments can vary substantially depending on spatial scale and assessment purpose, while Poussard et al. (2021) demonstrate that environmental inequalities in flood exposure are strongly scale-dependent. For social vulnerability measurement, Tate (2013), Schmidtlein et al. (2008), and Rufat et al. (2015, 2019) similarly caution that results depend on indicator selection, aggregation, and spatial context.

For this reason, the weak RP100 exposure-vulnerability association should not be read as evidence that social inequality is irrelevant for flood risk in the Elbe corridor. It shows that the chosen area-based, municipality-level exposure metric does not produce a simple regressive gradient. This finding prevents the thesis from forcing the Elbe case into an expected environmental-justice pattern. At the same time, it also clarifies the limits of area exposure as a justice indicator. The metric reveals where modeled flooding intersects municipal territory, but it does not fully capture the social consequences of flooding.

6.3 Exposure Curves and Residual Risk
The multi-return-period exposure curves add a temporal and probabilistic dimension to the interpretation. Most corridor municipalities are classified as early-exposure municipalities, meaning that a large share of their RP500 flooded area is already present by RP100 or earlier. This supports the use of RP100 as a meaningful benchmark in the Results chapter. The RP100 comparison is not simply an arbitrary snapshot that hides a fundamentally different social pattern at rarer return periods. Across vulnerability quintiles, the exposure curves preserve the same non-monotonic structure seen in the RP100 analysis.

At the same time, the exposure-curve results show why a single return period cannot capture all residual-risk dynamics. The delayed-jump municipalities form a small group, but they are substantively relevant because their exposure increases mainly after RP100. For these municipalities, a benchmark event can understate the spatial extent of exposure that appears under rarer events. This matters for flood-risk interpretation because return periods are not only technical categories. They shape how risk is communicated, how protection standards are understood, and how remaining risk beyond a design level becomes visible. Rojas et al. (2013) already show at European scale that adaptation and protection assumptions strongly influence projected flood damages, while the residual-risk literature stresses that protected areas can still face risk under events that exceed design standards or when protective structures fail (Tobin, 1995; Serra-Llobet et al., 2022; Fu et al., 2023).

The exposure curves should therefore be understood as complementary to the protection/loss data, not as a substitute for them. They describe how modeled flooded area accumulates across return periods. They do not directly identify dikes, walls, design standards, or the performance of specific protection infrastructure. This distinction matters because a late increase in area exposure may be shaped by many factors, including topography, floodplain geometry, raster modeling, or protection assumptions embedded in the hazard data. The curves are useful because they show whether exposure is already present under frequent or benchmark events or mainly appears under rarer events. But they cannot by themselves explain why that pattern occurs.

This reinforces one of the central conceptual claims of the thesis: protection should not be treated as a binary condition. A municipality is not simply protected or unprotected. It may show early area exposure but limited modeled losses, or it may show finite modeled loss return periods despite moderate area exposure. It may also appear as a no-event municipality in the provider portfolio while still lying within the EFAS/JRC RP500 corridor. These combinations are not contradictions. They reflect the fact that exposure, protection, and loss are different layers of flood risk.

6.4 Protection/Loss Results as the Stronger Justice-Relevant Signal
The provider protection/loss portfolio is the part of the empirical analysis where the justice-relevant social pattern becomes clearer. While RP100 area exposure is weakly and slightly negatively correlated with vulnerability, modeled annual loss probability is weakly but positively associated with vulnerability. Positive modeled losses and finite protection return-period estimates are also more common in the upper vulnerability groups than in the lowest-vulnerability group. Among the positive-loss municipalities, the median finite return period is substantially lower in the highest-vulnerability quintile than in the lowest-vulnerability quintile, even though the middle groups vary. Because lower return periods indicate more frequent modeled loss occurrence, this points toward weaker apparent protection-related outcomes among more vulnerable municipalities within that subset.

This pattern suggests that the social distribution of flood-related disadvantage is more visible in modeled loss/protection outcomes than in municipal area exposure alone. Flooded area is a necessary part of risk, but it is not sufficient to describe the social burden of flooding. A damage/loss portfolio incorporates additional information about where modeled flooding translates into losses. It is therefore closer to the kind of outcome that flood-justice debates are ultimately concerned with: not only whether a place intersects a flood footprint, but whether the modeled system produces losses, how frequently such losses occur, and how this relates to socio-economic vulnerability.

At the same time, the protection/loss result must be interpreted cautiously. The pattern is clearer than the exposure-vulnerability relationship, but it is not a perfect monotonic gradient. Q4 has the highest positive-loss share and mean annual loss probability, while Q5 remains high but does not exceed Q4 on every metric. The correlations are also moderate rather than strong. The results therefore do not prove that flood protection policy intentionally disadvantages more vulnerable municipalities. They also do not identify the causal history of protection investment or local settlement development. What they show is a descriptive distributive pattern: in the provider model output, upper vulnerability groups are more often associated with positive modeled losses and lower finite return periods than the lowest-vulnerability group.

This is still justice-relevant. Thaler and Hartmann (2016) argue that flood risk management always involves questions about who or what should be protected, while de Goër de Herve (2022) shows that distributive justice is one of the central justice dimensions in flood risk management. O’Hare and White (2018) similarly emphasize that flood disadvantage emerges where flood risk interacts with broader social disadvantage, and Moulds et al. (2021) show that flood-risk management can reproduce inequality under certain governance conditions. In this light, the Elbe results should be read as evidence of a potential distributive concern, not as proof of intentional unfairness. The concern is that municipalities with higher socio-economic vulnerability appear more likely to remain in the modeled loss-relevant part of the portfolio.

This distinction also structures the relationship between the two research questions. The first research question receives a limited answer: area exposure is highly uneven, but not strongly socially patterned. The second research question receives a more affirmative answer: modeled protection/loss outcomes show a clearer, though still cautious, association with vulnerability. The thesis therefore does not argue that exposure and protection tell the same story. It argues that the difference between them is itself the key empirical finding.

6.5 Interpreting No-Event Municipalities
The no-event municipalities are one of the most delicate parts of the analysis. The provider clarification makes clear that municipalities absent from the protection table were not removed because of an event-count threshold. They are absent because the provider flood portfolio does not produce modeled losses for them. In the thesis, they are therefore treated as no simulated loss event and modeled annual loss probability zero (N. Sairam and T. N. M. Phan, personal communication, July 2026). This interpretation is stronger than treating them as ordinary missing values, but it also requires careful limits.

First, no-event does not mean unprotected. It also does not mean that a municipality has no real-world flood risk. It means that no simulated loss event occurs in the provider model output used here. For large cities such as Hamburg or Magdeburg, the provider interpretation is that the modeled protection level is high enough that no simulated loss event appears in the event catalogue. This is a plausible interpretation within the provider model, and it is noteworthy because several prominent municipalities along the corridor appear as no-event cases rather than as finite-return-period cases.

Second, the no-event class also reflects model scope. The provider clarification states that smaller streams are not considered in the simulation, and Sairam et al. (2021) provide the broader process-based modeling context within which this limitation has to be understood. The stream-context diagnostic supports this caution. A large share of no-event municipalities is associated only with minor or other WFD water bodies, or with no intersecting WFD river body in the checked layer. This suggests that for many municipalities, absence from the positive-loss portfolio may be related to flood processes outside the represented river network. However, this is not the whole explanation, because a non-negligible group of no-event municipalities lies on or near the Elbe main stem or larger WFD river/tributary network. The no-event class therefore combines at least two interpretations: high modeled protection or no modeled losses in some places, and model-boundary limitations in others.

This mixed interpretation carries the thesis argument. If the absent municipalities had simply been treated as missing, the analysis would have ignored a meaningful model outcome. If they had been treated as risk-free, the analysis would have overstated what the provider data can show. The chosen interpretation is deliberately between these extremes. No-event municipalities are retained as zero modeled annual loss probability for probability-based summaries, but they are not assigned artificial finite protection return periods and are not described as having no flood risk. This preserves the information in the provider output while keeping the smaller-stream limitation visible.

There is also a broader justice implication here. Model visibility matters. Flood-risk governance depends heavily on what models represent, what they omit, and how their outputs are interpreted. If smaller streams are not represented, then some locally relevant flood processes may become less visible in large-scale protection/loss outputs. Conversely, if large cities appear as no-event cases because modeled protection prevents losses, this reveals the importance of high protection standards in shaping apparent risk. The no-event category therefore demonstrates that flood justice is not only about the distribution of observed or modeled risk. It is also about the distribution of visibility within the models used to describe risk.

6.6 Relation to German Flood Risk Management
The findings fit closely with the broader development of German flood risk management discussed in Chapter 2. German flood governance has moved from a narrow protection-oriented approach toward a more integrated risk-management perspective, especially after the major floods of 2002 and 2013 (Thieken et al., 2016; Grabs, 2016). This shift recognizes that structural protection remains important but cannot eliminate flood risk completely. The Elbe case illustrates exactly this tension. The corridor contains municipalities with extensive modeled area exposure, municipalities with no simulated losses in the provider portfolio, and municipalities with finite modeled protection/loss return periods. These outcomes cannot be reduced to a simple protected/unprotected distinction.

The results also resonate with the historical Elbe context. The Elbe is a heavily modified river system with long histories of settlement, diking, floodplain loss, and institutional learning (Grabs, 2016; Jüpner, 2018). In such a system, present-day flood-risk patterns are not only hydrological. They are shaped by past protection decisions, land-use development, and repeated flood experience. This helps explain why the spatial relationship between exposure and vulnerability is not straightforward. Areas with high floodplain exposure may also have strong protection or lower modeled losses, while other municipalities may remain loss-relevant despite not having the highest municipal flood shares.

At the same time, the results support the integrated risk-management argument that residual risk must remain central. No-event municipalities should not encourage a false interpretation of complete safety, and finite return periods should not be read as stable guarantees. The socio-hydrological literature warns that structural protection can reduce frequent losses while encouraging settlement, confidence, and asset accumulation in areas that remain exposed to rare or extreme events (Tobin, 1995; Serra-Llobet et al., 2022; Fu et al., 2023). German research similarly emphasizes that public protection needs to be complemented by spatial planning, preparedness, insurance, household adaptation, and risk communication (Osberghaus, 2015; Surminski and Thieken, 2017; Thieken et al., 2016).

The smaller-stream limitation also matters for German flood risk management. Large-river modeling is essential for a corridor-scale Elbe analysis, but local flood risk is not exhausted by the main stem or by larger represented river networks. Municipalities affected by smaller streams may still face relevant flood processes that are not fully captured in the provider portfolio. This limitation is not only technical. It has governance relevance because local risk perception, preparedness, and planning may depend on hazards that are less visible in large-scale datasets. The results therefore support a cautious interpretation of model-based protection and loss outputs within an integrated flood-risk-management framework.

6.7 Methodological Contribution
The main methodological contribution of the thesis is the separation of flood exposure, socio-economic vulnerability, and protection/loss outcomes within one municipality-level framework. Instead of collapsing these elements into a single risk score, the thesis compares them as distinct layers. This matters because the results show that different layers produce different justice-relevant patterns. Area exposure alone would suggest a highly uneven but weakly socially patterned flood geography. The protection/loss layer adds a more socially differentiated pattern. A single composite risk index could easily hide this difference.

The vulnerability-index approach also contributes to this separation. The final index is dimension-balanced and direction-coded, while PCA is used diagnostically rather than as the sole mechanical basis of the main score. This choice responds to the vulnerability-methods literature. Fekete (2009, 2010) shows the value of factor-analytic and indicator-based approaches for German river-flood vulnerability, but Tate (2013), Schmidtlein et al. (2008), and Rufat et al. (2015, 2019) caution that social vulnerability indices are sensitive to scale, variable selection, weighting, and validation. By preserving conceptual dimensions and avoiding a purely inductive PCA score as the main index, the thesis keeps the social interpretation of vulnerability more transparent.

The integration of multi-return-period exposure curves adds a further methodological layer. These curves do not solve the protection question by themselves, but they help prevent the analysis from relying on one return period alone. They show whether exposure is already present under more frequent or benchmark events or appears mainly under rarer events. Combined with the provider loss/protection portfolio, this allows the thesis to distinguish between area exposure dynamics and modeled loss/protection outcomes. This distinction is especially useful because flood-risk indicators can otherwise appear more coherent than they really are. The thesis demonstrates that the choice of indicator does not merely affect the size of an estimate; it can change the social interpretation of the flood-justice pattern.

Empirically, the contribution is therefore not that the thesis proves one simple inequality relationship for the Elbe corridor. Its contribution is that it identifies where such a relationship is weak, where it becomes clearer, and which data layers are responsible for that difference. This is a cautious contribution, but an analytically useful one. It shows that justice-oriented flood analysis should be designed to compare multiple dimensions of risk rather than assuming that one exposure metric can stand in for the whole problem.

6.8 Limitations and Critical Reflection
Several limitations follow directly from the design of the thesis. The first is the municipality-level ecological design. Municipalities are meaningful administrative and analytical units, but they are internally heterogeneous. The analysis cannot identify household-level exposure, neighborhood-scale segregation, or the social characteristics of the specific people and assets located inside the modeled flood footprint. It therefore cannot make individual-level claims about who is personally exposed or harmed. This is a substantial limitation because flood inequalities often operate at fine spatial scales. The municipality-level results should be read as evidence about socially differentiated local contexts, not as direct evidence about individual households.

The second limitation concerns the exposure metric. Flood share is measured as the proportion of municipal area covered by modeled flood depth cells. This is spatially consistent and transparent, but it is not depth-weighted, population-weighted, building-weighted, or asset-weighted. The metric also does not capture warning time, velocity, contamination, evacuation constraints, or other dimensions of flood severity. The EFAS/JRC hazard rasters used here are modeled large-scale river flood hazard maps, not official German hazard-zone maps (Baugh et al., 2024; Alfieri et al., 2014; Dottori et al., 2022). They are appropriate for consistent comparative exposure analysis, but they should not be interpreted as legal flood zones or as locally validated hydraulic studies for individual municipalities.

The third limitation concerns the vulnerability index. The index is based on INKAR indicators and on decisions about indicator selection, direction coding, imputation, dimension construction, and aggregation. These choices are documented and theoretically motivated, but they remain choices. Other defensible specifications could produce somewhat different spatial patterns. One corridor municipality, Berga-Wünschendorf, lacks a current INKAR match and therefore has no vulnerability-index value. More broadly, the index captures socio-economic vulnerability at the municipal level; it does not measure individual coping capacity, social networks, health status, household savings, insurance coverage, or previous flood experience directly.

The fourth limitation concerns the protection/loss data. The provider portfolio is not the same dataset as the EFAS/JRC exposure corridor. It represents municipalities with modeled losses in the provider flood portfolio and finite protection return-period estimates. The finite-return-period analysis is therefore restricted to the positive-loss subset, while no-event municipalities are treated as zero modeled annual loss probability only for probability-based summaries. This interpretation is supported by provider clarification, but it remains model-dependent. Smaller streams are not represented in the provider simulation, and this affects how no-event municipalities should be read. In addition, the thesis does not reconstruct the physical protection infrastructure, the age or design of dikes, local maintenance standards, or the political history of protection investment.

Finally, the thesis does not make a causal claim about policy intent or procedural justice. It cannot show why a municipality has a particular protection/loss outcome, whether investment decisions were fair, or how local stakeholders were included in flood-risk governance. The analysis is distributive, spatial, and descriptive. Its strength lies in showing how exposure, vulnerability, and modeled loss/protection outcomes align or fail to align across the Elbe corridor. Its limits are equally clear: it cannot explain all mechanisms behind those patterns, and it cannot replace detailed local case studies, hydrodynamic validation, or institutional analysis.

6.9 Implications for Flood Justice and Risk Governance
The main implication for flood justice is that a simple exposure-only reading is insufficient. The results do not support a strong claim that more vulnerable municipalities in the Elbe corridor are generally more exposed in terms of RP100 municipal flood share. However, they do support a more specific justice concern: more vulnerable municipalities appear more often in the modeled loss-relevant part of the provider portfolio, and among positive-loss municipalities they tend to have lower finite protection return periods. The justice issue therefore lies less in a uniform exposure gradient and more in the way exposure, vulnerability, modeled loss occurrence, and apparent protection-related outcomes combine.

For risk governance, this means that aggregate exposure indicators and protection standards should be interpreted carefully. A municipality with high area exposure is not automatically the most socially vulnerable or the most loss-relevant. A municipality with no simulated loss event is not automatically free of flood risk. A municipality with a finite protection return period is not simply unprotected, but it remains part of the modeled loss portfolio. These distinctions matter because flood-risk management decisions often depend on simplified categories of exposure, protection, and priority. The thesis shows that those categories can obscure important differences if they are not linked to social vulnerability and model limitations.

The results also underline that model boundaries are politically relevant. Flood-risk models help make risk governable, but they also define what is visible. The smaller-stream limitation is therefore not just a technical footnote. It affects which flood processes appear in the protection/loss analysis and which municipalities are interpreted as no-event cases. Similarly, high modeled protection in large cities may remove them from the positive-loss portfolio, even though their historical and potential flood relevance remains important. A justice-oriented flood-risk governance approach should therefore ask not only who is exposed or protected, but also whose risks are represented by the models used to allocate attention and resources.

Overall, the Elbe corridor illustrates why flood justice should be understood not as a search for one universal exposure gradient, but as an assessment of how different layers of risk, protection, and vulnerability become visible or invisible through the data and models used to describe them. The central finding is therefore deliberately cautious. Physical flood-area exposure is widespread and uneven, but not strongly regressive at municipality level. Modeled loss and protection-related outcomes, however, point to a clearer social differentiation. This combined result is the main basis for the concluding chapter: flood justice in the Elbe corridor cannot be reduced to where floodwater is modeled to go, but must also consider who remains socially vulnerable, where losses are modeled to occur, and how protection and model scope shape the visibility of residual risk.

7. Conclusion
This thesis examined whether modeled flood exposure and protection/loss-related outcomes in the Elbe flood corridor are distributed systematically across municipalities with different socio-economic vulnerability profiles. Starting from a distributive flood-justice perspective, it combined multi-return-period EFAS/JRC flood exposure, a municipality-level INKAR vulnerability index, and a provider damage/loss and protection return-period portfolio. The central finding is deliberately cautious: RP100 municipal flood-area exposure is highly uneven but only weakly associated with socio-economic vulnerability, while the modeled loss/protection layer reveals a clearer social differentiation.

This conclusion closes the thesis by returning to the two research questions, summarizing the contribution of the study, and outlining what the findings imply for flood justice and risk governance. It does not restate the full empirical analysis. Instead, it draws together the main argument that emerged across the preceding chapters: flood justice in the Elbe corridor cannot be assessed from flood-area exposure alone. It requires a combined reading of physical exposure, social vulnerability, modeled loss occurrence, apparent protection-related outcomes, and the model boundaries that shape which risks become visible.

7.1 Summary of Key Findings
The empirical analysis was based on an RP500-defined flood corridor of `835` municipalities. Flood exposure was available for all municipalities in this corridor, while the final socio-economic vulnerability index was available for `834` municipalities because Berga-Wünschendorf lacked a current INKAR match in the processed data. The protection/loss portfolio formed a third and analytically different layer: `280` corridor municipalities had positive modeled losses and finite protection return-period estimates, while `555` municipalities were interpreted, following provider clarification, as no simulated loss event and zero modeled annual loss probability in the provider portfolio. These different denominators are central to the interpretation of the thesis, because exposure, vulnerability, and finite protection return periods do not describe the same empirical object.

The first research question asked how modeled flood exposure is distributed across municipalities with different socio-economic vulnerability profiles in the Elbe flood corridor. The answer is a limited negative finding. RP100 municipal flood-area exposure is highly uneven, but it is not socially patterned in a simple regressive way at municipality level. The correlation between the final vulnerability index and RP100 flood share is close to zero, with both Pearson and Spearman correlations at approximately `-0.050`. The vulnerability-quintile comparison is also non-monotonic. The lowest-vulnerability quintile has the highest mean RP100 flood share, while the highest-vulnerability quintile remains meaningfully exposed but does not form the corridor-wide exposure maximum.

The thesis therefore does not find evidence for a simple pattern in which more vulnerable municipalities are generally more exposed in terms of municipal flood-area share. High-exposure and high-vulnerability municipalities do exist, and they remain relevant from a justice-oriented perspective. However, they do not dominate the high-exposure group. The highest RP100 exposure tercile includes low-, medium-, and high-vulnerability municipalities. The exposure result is therefore spatially unequal, but not straightforwardly regressive. This finding prevents the thesis from forcing the Elbe corridor into an expected environmental-justice pattern that the area-exposure data do not support.

The multi-return-period exposure analysis strengthens this conclusion. Most corridor municipalities are early-exposure cases: `772` of `835` municipalities, or about `92.5%`, already realize a large share of their RP500 exposure by RP100 or earlier. This supports the use of RP100 as a meaningful benchmark for the main exposure-vulnerability comparison. At the same time, the smaller group of delayed-jump municipalities shows why a single return period cannot capture all residual-risk dynamics. The exposure curves therefore complement the RP100 analysis. They show that the non-monotonic vulnerability pattern is not simply an artifact of one benchmark return period, while also preserving information about municipalities where exposure increases mainly under rarer events.

The second research question asked how modeled protection and loss-related outcomes relate to flood exposure and socio-economic vulnerability across the corridor. Here the answer is more affirmative, though still cautious. The provider protection/loss portfolio shows a clearer social pattern than the RP100 area-exposure layer. Positive modeled losses and finite protection return-period estimates are more common in the upper vulnerability groups than in the lowest-vulnerability group: the share is `16.2%` in the lowest-vulnerability quintile, `46.7%` in Q4, and `41.0%` in the highest-vulnerability quintile. When no-event municipalities are retained as zero modeled annual loss probability, the association between vulnerability and annual loss probability is weak but positive.

Among positive-loss municipalities, finite protection return periods also point toward weaker apparent protection-related outcomes among more vulnerable municipalities. The median finite return period is `156` years in the lowest-vulnerability quintile and `28` years in the highest-vulnerability quintile, with some variation in the middle groups. This does not create a perfectly monotonic staircase, and it does not prove intentional unequal protection policy. It does show, however, that the loss/protection layer is more socially differentiated than the area-exposure layer alone. The second research question is therefore answered by a clearer, though still descriptive, distributive pattern.

The interpretation of no-event municipalities is central to this result. These municipalities are not ordinary missing values, and they are not interpreted as places without real-world flood risk. They are municipalities with no simulated loss event in the provider flood portfolio and are therefore retained as zero modeled annual loss probability for probability-based summaries. For some larger cities, this may reflect high modeled protection levels; for other municipalities, especially those associated with smaller streams, it may reflect model scope. This distinction matters because the no-event class is both a meaningful model outcome and a limitation-sensitive category. It shows why protection/loss data must be interpreted together with model assumptions rather than as a simple inventory of protected and unprotected municipalities.

7.2 Contributions of the Thesis
The first contribution of the thesis is empirical. It provides municipality-level evidence for the flood-relevant Elbe corridor rather than treating the Elbe basin as one undifferentiated unit. This corridor-based design makes the analysis more closely aligned with modeled flood exposure while preserving the broader Elbe basin as the historical and governance context of the study. The result is an empirical picture that is more specific than a basin-wide average but broader than a single local case study.

The second contribution is methodological. The thesis combines three layers that are often considered separately: multi-return-period modeled flood exposure, socio-economic vulnerability, and modeled loss/protection outcomes. Keeping these layers analytically distinct is central to the thesis. If they had been collapsed into one composite risk score, the difference between the weakly patterned area-exposure result and the more socially differentiated protection/loss result would have been much less visible. The analysis shows that the choice of flood-risk indicator does not merely change the size of an estimate; it can change the justice-relevant interpretation of the whole pattern.

The vulnerability-index construction is part of this methodological contribution. The final index is direction-coded and dimension-balanced, while PCA is used as a diagnostic tool rather than as the mechanical basis of the main score. This responds to the vulnerability-methods literature, which emphasizes that social vulnerability indices are sensitive to scale, indicator selection, aggregation, and validation (Fekete, 2009; Rufat et al., 2015; Tate, 2013). The thesis therefore treats the vulnerability index as a structured analytical tool rather than as a direct measure of individual household vulnerability. This makes the index useful for municipality-level distributive comparison while keeping its limits visible.

The third contribution is conceptual. The thesis demonstrates that flood justice cannot be reduced to a single exposure gradient. In the Elbe corridor, municipal flood-area exposure, modeled annual loss probability, finite protection return periods, and no-event status do not simply reproduce one another. This matters for distributive flood-justice analysis because it shows that a conclusion based on area exposure alone would miss an important part of the social pattern. The thesis contributes not by demonstrating one universal inequality relationship, but by showing how the distributive pattern changes when the analysis moves from exposure to modeled loss and protection-related outcomes.

7.3 Reflections on Flood Justice and Risk Governance
The findings have two main implications for flood justice. First, they show that an exposure-only reading is not enough. The results do not support a strong claim that more vulnerable municipalities in the Elbe corridor are generally more exposed in terms of RP100 municipal flood share. However, they do support a combined justice concern around modeled loss occurrence, apparent protection-related outcomes, residual risk, and model visibility. This fits the broader flood-justice literature, which treats flood risk management as a question of how burdens, benefits, protection, and remaining risks are distributed across social and spatial contexts (de Goër de Herve, 2022; Thaler and Hartmann, 2016).

Second, the findings show why protection should not be treated as binary safety. No-event municipalities, finite return periods, and exposure curves all point to a more complex risk landscape. A municipality with high area exposure is not automatically the most loss-relevant or the most socially vulnerable. A municipality with no simulated loss event is not automatically free of flood risk. A municipality with a finite protection return period is not simply unprotected, but it remains part of the modeled loss portfolio. These distinctions matter because flood-risk governance often relies on categories that simplify exposure and protection. The thesis shows that such categories need to be interpreted alongside social vulnerability and residual-risk visibility.

The role of model boundaries is especially significant. The provider clarification that smaller streams are not fully represented means that some locally relevant flood processes may be less visible in the protection/loss portfolio. This is not only a technical limitation. It affects how risk is seen, summarized, and potentially prioritized. At the same time, the presence of no-event cases among larger cities shows that high modeled protection can also remove municipalities from the positive-loss subset. Flood-risk governance therefore depends not only on the physical distribution of water, but also on the models through which risk and protection are made legible.

For practical risk governance, the implication is modest but relevant. Planning, communication, and prioritization should not rely only on aggregate flood extent or on formal protection categories. They should consider how exposure, vulnerability, modeled loss occurrence, and model scope interact. In this sense, the thesis supports an integrated flood-risk-management perspective: structural protection remains central, but it does not eliminate residual risk, and it does not remove the need to consider social vulnerability, preparedness, local flood processes, and risk communication.

7.4 Outlook for Research and Practice
Several extensions follow directly from the limits of this thesis. The most immediate next step would be to move beyond area-based exposure and construct population-weighted, settlement-weighted, building-weighted, and depth-weighted exposure indicators. This would help distinguish municipalities with large flooded territories from municipalities where people, buildings, or assets are actually concentrated inside the modeled flood footprint. Such extensions would be especially useful for the high-high municipalities identified in the bivariate exposure-vulnerability map and for municipalities with high EFAS/JRC exposure but no simulated loss events in the provider portfolio.

A second extension concerns the protection/loss data. The provider exposure-domain list mentioned in the provider communication should be compared directly with the RP500 corridor once available. This would help separate no-event cases that reflect high modeled protection from cases that reflect differences in model domain, represented river network, or smaller-stream exclusion. Local protection-infrastructure data could also be used to validate the interpretation of finite return periods and no-event municipalities. Such validation would be particularly valuable for larger cities and lower-Elbe municipalities where high modeled protection and model-boundary issues may overlap.

A third extension would be to move below the municipality scale. The municipality-level design is defensible for integrating the available data, but it cannot capture within-municipality sorting or household-level exposure. Selected case studies could examine high-exposure/high-vulnerability municipalities, delayed-jump municipalities, and no-event cities with substantial EFAS/JRC flood shares. These case studies could combine official hazard maps, local protection information, settlement data, household vulnerability, insurance, preparedness, and recovery capacity. They could also extend the present distributive analysis toward procedural and recognition justice by asking how local actors understand and participate in flood-risk governance.

Future research could also examine how the observed patterns change under climate scenarios or alternative adaptation assumptions. The present thesis is based on current modeled hazard and provider loss/protection outputs. It does not estimate future climate-driven changes in flood frequency, future land-use development, or future protection investment. A dynamic extension could examine whether the weak exposure-vulnerability relationship and the clearer protection/loss differentiation persist under future scenarios. This would connect the corridor-level justice analysis more directly to long-term adaptation planning.

For practice, the main recommendation is to use flood-risk indicators in combination rather than in isolation. Flood extent maps, vulnerability indices, loss models, and protection estimates each reveal different parts of the risk landscape. None of them should be treated as a complete representation on its own. No-event classes should be interpreted as model outcomes that require explanation, not as automatically safe places. Residual risk should be communicated even where modeled protection appears high. Smaller streams and local flood processes should remain visible in planning, especially where large-scale river-flood models do not fully represent them.

7.5 Final Remarks
The thesis began from the premise that floods are not only physical events, and that flood losses and protection outcomes are shaped by social and political decisions. The Elbe corridor supports this premise, but in a more cautious way than a simple exposure-inequality expectation would suggest. At municipality level, physical flood-area exposure is widespread and uneven, but not strongly regressive. The stronger justice-relevant signal appears when modeled loss occurrence, protection-related return periods, socio-economic vulnerability, and model limitations are considered together.

The central message of the thesis is therefore not that one social group or one type of municipality carries all flood-related disadvantage. Rather, it is that flood justice requires attention to the layers through which risk is produced, reduced, modeled, and made visible. Exposure, vulnerability, protection, loss, and model scope each tell part of the story. Analyzing them separately, and then bringing them into relation, makes it possible to see patterns that a single exposure map would miss.

Flood justice in the Elbe corridor is therefore not a single map of exposure, but a question of how exposure, vulnerability, protection, loss, and model visibility are brought into relation.
