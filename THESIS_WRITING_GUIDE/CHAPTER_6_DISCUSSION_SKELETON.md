# Chapter 6 Discussion Skeleton

Purpose:

Chapter 6 should interpret the empirical pattern from Chapter 5. It should not repeat all results and should not introduce a new analysis. The core task is to explain what the combination of weak exposure-vulnerability association and clearer protection/loss differentiation means for flood justice, residual risk, and the interpretation of municipality-level flood-risk data.

## Core Argument of Chapter 6

The discussion should be built around one central argument:

The Elbe corridor does not show a simple regressive pattern in municipal RP100 area exposure, but the provider loss/protection layer points to a more socially differentiated pattern of modeled loss occurrence and finite protection return periods. Flood justice in this case therefore cannot be assessed from flood-area exposure alone. It depends on how physical exposure, socio-economic vulnerability, protection, modeled loss occurrence, and model limitations are interpreted together.

This is the bridge from Chapter 5:

- RP100 exposure-vulnerability correlation is close to zero.
- High-high municipalities exist but do not dominate the corridor.
- Exposure curves show that most exposure is already realized by RP100.
- Positive modeled loss and finite protection return-period estimates are more common in upper vulnerability groups.
- Among positive-loss municipalities, median finite return periods are lower in the highest vulnerability groups.
- No-event municipalities are not missing values, but no simulated loss events / zero modeled annual loss probability in the provider portfolio.
- Smaller streams remain a key model limitation.

## 6.1 Interpreting the Main Empirical Pattern

Function:

Open the discussion by restating the empirical puzzle in interpretive terms.

Main points:

- The results do not support a simple "more vulnerable = more exposed" narrative for RP100 municipal area exposure.
- This does not make the justice question disappear.
- Instead, it shifts the focus from a single exposure metric to the relation between exposure, vulnerability, modeled losses, and protection-related outcomes.
- The key empirical contribution is the difference between the exposure layer and the loss/protection layer.

Possible paragraph logic:

1. Start with the central finding.
2. Explain why it is not a null finding.
3. Introduce the need to interpret exposure and protection/loss separately.
4. Point forward to residual risk and model visibility.

Anchor citations:

- Poussard et al. (2021) for scale dependence of flood inequalities
- Odersky and Löffler (2024) for German local-scale regressive flood exposure
- Qiang (2019) for broader exposure-disparity framing
- de Goer de Herve (2022), Thaler and Hartmann (2016) for flood-justice framing

## 6.2 Why RP100 Area Exposure Does Not Show a Simple Social Gradient

Function:

Discuss the weak and slightly negative RP100 exposure-vulnerability relationship without overexplaining it causally.

Main points:

- Municipal area exposure is not the same as population exposure, settlement exposure, asset exposure, or damage.
- Low-vulnerability municipalities can be highly exposed if large municipal territories include floodplain areas.
- High-vulnerability municipalities can be less exposed at RP100 but still vulnerable in terms of adaptive capacity and modeled losses.
- The municipality scale smooths internal social and spatial variation.
- The corridor definition selects flood-relevant municipalities, but the resulting administrative units vary strongly in size, settlement structure, and land-use composition.

Interpretive line:

The weak relationship may partly reflect the metric and scale rather than a true absence of social differentiation in flood risk.

Anchor citations:

- de Moel et al. (2015) for scale and flood-risk assessment differences
- Poussard et al. (2021) for spatial-scale effects in flood inequalities
- Hinojos et al. (2023) for MAUP / scale effects if used
- Rufat et al. (2015, 2019) and Tate (2013) for indicator/index caution

## 6.3 Exposure Curves and Residual Risk

Function:

Interpret why the exposure-curve results matter even after receiving the protection dataset.

Main points:

- The curve typology shows that most municipalities are early-exposure cases.
- RP100 is therefore a meaningful benchmark for much of the corridor.
- Delayed-jump municipalities are a small but substantively interesting group because their exposure increases mainly after RP100.
- Exposure curves do not directly measure protection infrastructure.
- They help describe how modeled flooded area accumulates across return periods and therefore complement the provider protection/loss data.

Interpretive line:

The exposure curves show that residual-risk questions cannot be reduced to a single return period, but they also do not replace modeled loss/protection information.

Anchor citations:

- Rojas et al. (2013) for return-period/adaptation logic
- de Moel et al. (2015) for flood-risk assessment scale and method
- Tobin (1995), Serra-Llobet et al. (2022), Fu et al. (2023) for residual risk and protection limits

## 6.4 Protection/Loss Results as the Stronger Justice-Relevant Signal

Function:

Interpret why the protection/loss portfolio shows a clearer social pattern than RP100 area exposure.

Main points:

- Positive modeled losses are more common in upper vulnerability groups than in the lowest-vulnerability group.
- Full-corridor modeled annual loss probability has a weak positive vulnerability association.
- Among positive-loss municipalities, finite protection return periods decline with higher vulnerability.
- This suggests that the socially relevant pattern emerges more clearly after moving from area exposure to modeled loss/protection outcomes.
- However, this should be framed as a descriptive pattern, not a causal proof of unequal policy intent.

Interpretive line:

The social distribution of flood-related disadvantage appears more visible in modeled loss/protection outcomes than in municipal flood-area exposure alone.

Anchor citations:

- Thaler and Hartmann (2016) for the question of who is protected
- de Goer de Herve (2022) for distributive flood-justice dimensions
- O'Hare and White (2018) for flood disadvantage
- Moulds et al. (2021) for flood-risk management and inequality
- Tovar Reanos (2021) for welfare and flood inequality in Germany

## 6.5 Interpreting No-Event Municipalities

Function:

This must be a careful section because it is the most delicate part of the protection data.

Main points:

- No-event municipalities are not missing at random.
- They are not unprotected municipalities.
- They are coded as zero modeled annual loss probability following provider clarification.
- For larger cities, no-event status may reflect high modeled protection levels.
- For smaller streams, no-event status may reflect model scope because smaller streams are not represented.
- This creates a mixed interpretation: some no-event municipalities are protected/no-loss cases; others may be outside the represented flood/loss process.

Interpretive line:

The no-event class is meaningful, but it combines substantive modeled protection outcomes with model-boundary effects.

Anchor citations and evidence:

- N. Sairam and T. N. M. Phan, personal communication, July 2026
- Sairam et al. (2021) for process-based flood-risk modelling and limitations
- `NO_LOSS_STREAM_CONTEXT_CHECK.md` and Figure/map output for the stream-context diagnostic

Important caution:

Do not write "Hamburg has no flood risk". Write: "Hamburg appears as a no-event case in the provider simulation, which the provider interprets as sufficiently high modeled protection to prevent simulated losses in the event catalogue."

## 6.6 Relation to German Flood Risk Management

Function:

Connect the empirical results back to the German FRM and Elbe context from Chapter 2.

Main points:

- German flood risk management has moved toward integrated risk management, but structural protection remains central.
- The results show why protection cannot be treated as binary safety.
- No-event cities and finite-RP municipalities both demonstrate that protection/loss outcomes are model-dependent and uneven.
- Smaller-stream limitations matter because local flood risk is not exhausted by large-river modelling.
- The Elbe case reflects the broader German tension between protection standards, residual risk, and local vulnerability.

Anchor citations:

- Thieken et al. (2016)
- Sairam et al. (2021)
- Grabs (2016)
- Juepner (2018)
- Surminski and Thieken (2017)
- Osberghaus (2015)

## 6.7 Methodological Contribution

Function:

State what the thesis contributes methodologically and empirically.

Main points:

- It combines EFAS/JRC multi-return-period exposure with municipality-level INKAR vulnerability and provider loss/protection data.
- It separates exposure, vulnerability, and protection/loss outcomes instead of collapsing them into one opaque risk score.
- It uses a dimension-balanced vulnerability index and keeps PCA as a diagnostic rather than letting PCA mechanically define the main index.
- It shows how different flood-risk indicators can produce different justice-relevant patterns.

Interpretive line:

The thesis contributes not by proving one simple inequality pattern, but by showing how the pattern changes when moving from exposure to modeled loss/protection.

Anchor citations:

- Fekete (2009, 2010)
- Schmidtlein et al. (2008)
- Tate (2013)
- Rufat et al. (2015, 2019)
- Poussard et al. (2021)

## 6.8 Limitations and Critical Reflection

Function:

Be candid and precise. This section should make the thesis more defensible, not weaker.

Limitations to cover:

- municipality-level ecological design; no household or neighbourhood inference
- area-based flood share; not depth-weighted, population-weighted, building-weighted, or damage-weighted
- EFAS/JRC maps are modeled, large-scale river flood hazard maps, not official German hazard maps
- RP100 benchmark is useful but not exhaustive
- vulnerability index depends on indicator choice, direction coding, imputation, dimension balancing, and INKAR coverage
- one municipality lacks INKAR vulnerability data
- protection/loss data are derived from provider model portfolio and cover positive-loss municipalities differently from the EFAS corridor
- no-event interpretation depends on provider clarification
- smaller streams are not represented in the provider simulation
- finite return-period analysis applies only to the positive-loss subset
- no causal inference about policy intent, investment decisions, or procedural justice
- possible lower-Elbe/tidal/coastal process mismatch should be noted if still uncertain

Anchor citations:

- Tate (2013)
- Schmidtlein et al. (2008)
- Rufat et al. (2019)
- de Moel et al. (2015)
- Sairam et al. (2021)
- Dottori et al. (2022) / Alfieri et al. (2014) for hazard dataset limitations

## 6.9 Implications for Flood Justice and Risk Governance

Function:

Close the discussion by returning to the thesis question and setting up the Conclusion.

Main points:

- The results do not support a simple distributive injustice claim based only on RP100 municipal area exposure.
- They do support a more nuanced justice concern around modeled loss occurrence, apparent protection-related outcomes, and residual risk.
- Justice analysis should distinguish physical exposure, social vulnerability, modeled loss, and protection.
- Risk governance should not rely only on aggregate exposure or protection standards.
- Model boundaries matter politically because what is not represented may become less visible in planning.
- The smaller-stream limitation is therefore not just technical; it affects what kinds of flood risk are visible in the analysis.

Possible final sentence:

The Elbe corridor therefore illustrates why flood justice should be understood not as a search for one universal exposure gradient, but as an assessment of how different layers of risk, protection, and vulnerability become visible or invisible through the data and models used to govern them.
