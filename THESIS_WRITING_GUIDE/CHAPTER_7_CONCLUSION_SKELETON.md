# Chapter 7 Conclusion Skeleton

Purpose:

Chapter 7 should close the thesis cleanly. It should answer the two research questions, state the contribution, reflect briefly on flood justice and risk governance, and identify realistic next steps for research and practice. It should not introduce new analysis, new figures, new datasets, or a new literature debate.

The conclusion should be shorter and more decisive than Chapter 6. Chapter 6 interprets; Chapter 7 closes.

## Core Closing Argument

The conclusion should be built around one final argument:

The Elbe corridor does not show a simple regressive pattern in municipal RP100 flood-area exposure, but the protection/loss portfolio reveals a more socially differentiated pattern of modeled loss occurrence and apparent protection-related outcomes. Flood justice in this thesis is therefore not demonstrated by one exposure gradient. It emerges from comparing physical exposure, socio-economic vulnerability, modeled losses, protection-related return periods, and the model boundaries that shape which risks become visible.

This wording keeps the result strong but defensible. It avoids claiming that "poor municipalities are simply more exposed" or that "rich municipalities are simply better protected." The thesis story is more nuanced and more interesting than that.

## Required Answers to the Research Questions

### Research Question 1

Question:

How is modeled flood exposure distributed across municipalities with different socio-economic vulnerability profiles in the Elbe flood corridor?

Answer:

Modeled municipal flood-area exposure is highly uneven across the RP500 Elbe corridor, but it is not strongly or linearly associated with socio-economic vulnerability. The RP100 exposure-vulnerability relationship is close to zero, the vulnerability-quintile pattern is non-monotonic, and high-exposure municipalities occur across low, medium, and high vulnerability groups. High-high municipalities exist and matter, but they do not dominate the high-exposure group.

Evidence to preserve:

- RP500 corridor: `835` municipalities.
- RP100 exposure-vulnerability correlation: Pearson and Spearman both approximately `-0.050`.
- Lowest-vulnerability quintile has the highest mean RP100 flood share: `35.6%`.
- Highest-vulnerability quintile remains meaningfully exposed: mean RP100 flood share `27.2%`.
- High RP100 exposure tercile contains `104` low-vulnerability, `81` medium-vulnerability, and `93` high-vulnerability municipalities.
- Exposure curves confirm that most municipalities are early-exposure cases: `772` of `835`, or about `92.5%`.

Interpretive sentence:

The first research question is therefore answered with a negative but meaningful finding: flood-area exposure is spatially unequal, but it is not socially patterned in a simple regressive way at municipality level.

### Research Question 2

Question:

How do modeled protection and loss-related outcomes relate to flood exposure and socio-economic vulnerability across this corridor?

Answer:

The protection/loss portfolio shows a clearer social pattern than the RP100 area-exposure layer. Positive modeled losses and finite protection return-period estimates are more common in the upper vulnerability groups than in the lowest-vulnerability group. When no-event municipalities are retained as zero modeled annual loss probability, the association between vulnerability and annual loss probability is weak but positive. Among positive-loss municipalities, finite return periods are substantially lower in the highest-vulnerability quintile than in the lowest-vulnerability quintile, although the middle groups do not form a perfectly monotonic sequence.

Evidence to preserve:

- Provider table: `301` municipalities total.
- Matched corridor positive-loss municipalities: `280`.
- No-event / zero modeled annual loss probability municipalities: `555`.
- Positive modeled loss share by vulnerability quintile:
  - Q1: `16.2%`
  - Q2: `30.5%`
  - Q3: `33.5%`
  - Q4: `46.7%`
  - Q5: `41.0%`
- Mean modeled annual loss probability:
  - Q1: `0.9%`
  - Q4: `4.8%`
  - Q5: `3.3%`
- Vulnerability vs annual loss probability, full corridor:
  - Pearson: `0.118`
  - Spearman: `0.230`
- Median finite protection return period among positive-loss municipalities:
  - Q1: `156` years
  - Q5: `28` years
  - with middle-group variation: Q2 `61`, Q3 `68`, Q4 `40`.

Interpretive sentence:

The second research question is therefore answered more affirmatively: modeled loss and protection-related outcomes are more socially differentiated than area exposure alone, but the pattern remains descriptive and should not be read as causal evidence of intentional unequal protection policy.

## 7. Conclusion

Function:

Open with a concise return to the problem: flood risk is not only about hazard, but about the social distribution of exposure, protection, and residual risk. Then state that the thesis examined this issue for the RP500 Elbe flood corridor at municipality level.

Suggested opening logic:

1. Return to the central puzzle from the Introduction.
2. Name the empirical design in one sentence.
3. State the final answer in one clear sentence.

Possible opening paragraph:

This thesis examined whether modeled flood exposure and protection/loss-related outcomes in the Elbe flood corridor are distributed systematically across municipalities with different socio-economic vulnerability profiles. Starting from a distributive flood-justice perspective, it combined multi-return-period EFAS/JRC flood exposure, a municipality-level INKAR vulnerability index, and a provider damage/loss and protection return-period portfolio. The central finding is deliberately nuanced: RP100 municipal flood-area exposure is highly uneven but only weakly associated with socio-economic vulnerability, while the modeled loss/protection layer reveals a clearer social differentiation.

Do not:

- Reopen all theory.
- Repeat every number from Chapter 5.
- Add new figures.
- Overstate the protection results as causal proof.

## 7.1 Summary of Key Findings

Function:

Answer both research questions clearly and compactly.

Suggested paragraph structure:

Paragraph 1:

Restate sample and analytical layers. Mention `835` corridor municipalities, `834` vulnerability records, `280` positive-loss municipalities, and `555` no-event municipalities only if needed for clarity. This paragraph should make clear that the denominators differ across exposure, vulnerability, and finite-protection analyses.

Paragraph 2:

Answer RQ1. RP100 area exposure is uneven but not simply regressive. The correlation is close to zero, the quintile pattern is non-monotonic, and high exposure exists across the vulnerability distribution. High-high cases exist but do not dominate.

Paragraph 3:

Summarize exposure curves. Most municipalities are early-exposure cases, so RP100 is a meaningful benchmark for much of the corridor. Delayed-jump cases remain important because they show why a single return period cannot capture all residual-risk dynamics.

Paragraph 4:

Answer RQ2. The protection/loss layer shows the stronger social signal. Positive modeled losses are more common in upper vulnerability groups, annual loss probability is weakly positively associated with vulnerability, and the positive-loss subset shows lower finite return periods in the highest-vulnerability group than in the lowest.

Paragraph 5:

Clarify no-event interpretation. No-event municipalities are not ordinary missing values and not risk-free places. They are zero modeled annual loss probability in the provider portfolio, with high modeled protection explaining some larger-city cases and smaller-stream model scope remaining an important limitation.

## 7.2 Contributions of the Thesis

Function:

State what the thesis contributes empirically, methodologically, and conceptually.

Suggested paragraph structure:

Paragraph 1: Empirical contribution.

The thesis provides municipality-level evidence for the flood-relevant Elbe corridor rather than a full-basin average or a single-event case study. It shows that corridor-scale flood justice cannot be reduced to simple spatial exposure overlap.

Paragraph 2: Methodological contribution.

The thesis combines three layers that are often treated separately: EFAS/JRC multi-return-period exposure, INKAR-based socio-economic vulnerability, and modeled loss/protection outcomes. It keeps them analytically separate rather than collapsing them into one opaque risk score.

Paragraph 3: Vulnerability-index contribution.

The thesis uses a dimension-balanced vulnerability index and PCA diagnostics. This responds to the vulnerability-methods literature by keeping indicator choices transparent and avoiding the mechanical dominance of one covariance structure.

Paragraph 4: Conceptual contribution.

The thesis shows that different flood-risk indicators can tell different justice stories. Area exposure, modeled loss probability, and finite protection return periods do not simply reproduce one another.

Literature anchors if needed:

- `Poussard et al. (2021)` for scale-sensitive exposure inequality.
- `Fekete (2009, 2010)`, `Tate (2013)`, `Rufat et al. (2015, 2019)`, `Schmidtlein et al. (2008)` for vulnerability-index caution.
- `de Goër de Herve (2022)` and `Thaler and Hartmann (2016)` for distributive flood justice.

Suggested contribution sentence:

The thesis contributes not by demonstrating one universal inequality gradient, but by showing how the distributive pattern changes when the analysis moves from municipal flood-area exposure to modeled loss and protection-related outcomes.

## 7.3 Reflections on Flood Justice and Risk Governance

Function:

Close the normative part of the thesis without making it too policy-prescriptive. This section should translate the empirical findings into flood-justice and governance meaning.

Suggested paragraph structure:

Paragraph 1:

Flood justice should not be understood as a search for one single exposure gradient. In the Elbe corridor, the justice-relevant pattern appears through the combination of exposure, vulnerability, modeled losses, and protection-related outcomes.

Paragraph 2:

Risk governance should avoid treating protection as binary safety. No-event municipalities, finite return periods, and exposure curves all show that protection and residual risk are model-dependent and uneven.

Paragraph 3:

Model boundaries matter. Smaller streams not represented in the provider simulation are not just a technical limitation, because they affect what kinds of flood risk become visible in large-scale outputs.

Paragraph 4:

The governance implication should be modest: planning and risk communication should not rely only on aggregate exposure or formal protection categories. They should consider social vulnerability and residual-risk visibility together.

Literature anchors if needed:

- `Thaler and Hartmann (2016)` for protection as an allocation question.
- `de Goër de Herve (2022)` for distributive justice in FRM.
- `Tobin (1995)`, `Serra-Llobet et al. (2022)`, `Fu et al. (2023)` for residual risk behind protection.
- `Sairam et al. (2021)` for model characteristics and limitations.

Avoid:

- Strong policy recommendations not supported by the thesis.
- Claims about specific investment failures.
- Claims that municipalities with no modeled losses are safe.

## 7.4 Outlook for Research and Practice

Function:

State what should happen next. This should be concrete, not generic.

Research outlook:

- Build population-weighted, building-weighted, settlement-weighted, and depth-weighted exposure metrics.
- Compare EFAS/JRC modeled exposure with official German hazard maps where available.
- Integrate provider exposure-domain lists once received, to distinguish no-event due to high protection from no-event due to model domain or smaller-stream exclusion.
- Validate protection/loss patterns with local protection infrastructure data, where available.
- Move below municipality level for selected case studies, especially high-high municipalities and no-event cities with high EFAS/JRC exposure.
- Explore whether the pattern changes under climate scenarios or future adaptation assumptions.
- Include household, insurance, preparedness, and recovery data if available.
- Examine procedural and recognition justice through qualitative or institutional case studies.

Practice outlook:

- Use vulnerability and protection/loss indicators together rather than relying on flood extent alone.
- Treat no-event classes as model outcomes that need interpretation, not as automatically safe places.
- Communicate residual risk in areas with high modeled protection.
- Pay attention to smaller streams and local flood processes that may be less visible in large-scale river-flood models.

Literature anchors if needed:

- `de Moel et al. (2015)` for scale and flood-risk assessment.
- `Poussard et al. (2021)` for scale-sensitive inequality.
- `Sairam et al. (2021)` for process-based modelling.
- `Surminski and Thieken (2017)`, `Osberghaus (2015)`, `Thieken et al. (2016)` for integrated FRM, insurance, preparedness, and household adaptation.

Do not:

- Promise analyses the thesis did not do.
- Write as if all future work is equally important. Prioritize the most direct extensions of this thesis.

Recommended priority order:

1. Population/settlement/depth-weighted exposure.
2. Provider exposure-domain comparison and smaller-stream clarification.
3. Local validation of protection infrastructure and modeled no-event cases.
4. Sub-municipal or case-study analysis of high-high and no-event cities.
5. Climate/adaptation scenario extension.

## 7.5 Final Remarks

Function:

End with a clean, memorable final paragraph. It should echo the Introduction without becoming rhetorical.

Possible closing logic:

1. Floods are not only physical events; losses and protection outcomes are socially produced.
2. The Elbe corridor shows why flood justice requires layered evidence.
3. The thesis does not find one simple injustice gradient, but it does show that exposure-only analysis misses important social differentiation.
4. End on the value of making exposure, vulnerability, protection, and model visibility analytically distinct.

Possible final paragraph:

The Elbe corridor shows that flood justice cannot be reduced to the question of where modeled floodwater is located. At municipality level, physical exposure is uneven but not strongly regressive. The stronger justice-relevant signal appears when modeled loss occurrence, protection-related return periods, socio-economic vulnerability, and model limitations are considered together. This layered result is the central message of the thesis: flood-risk governance needs indicators that make both social vulnerability and residual-risk visibility explicit, because what is not distinguished analytically can easily disappear politically.

Final sentence option:

Flood justice in the Elbe corridor is therefore not a single map of exposure, but a question of how exposure, vulnerability, protection, loss, and model visibility are brought into relation.
