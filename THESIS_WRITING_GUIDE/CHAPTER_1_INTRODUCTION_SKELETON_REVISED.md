# 1. INTRODUCTION

## Critical logic check before writing

### Does the original Chapter 1 structure basically work?
- `Yes`, the basic logic works.
- `No`, it should not remain exactly as before.

### What should be adjusted?
- `Motivation` should become **Motivation & Problem Framing**.
  - Reason: the introduction should not only motivate the topic, but already frame the concrete puzzle.
- `Research Gap & Objective` should become **Research Gap & Thesis Contribution**.
  - Reason: “objective” is slightly too generic; what the reader really needs is the specific contribution.
- `Research Questions & Hypothesis` should become **Research Questions & Analytical Orientation**.
  - Reason: a rigid hypothesis is less suitable for the current staged and distributive design. A cautious working expectation is still possible, but the section should primarily orient the reader.
- Add a short final subsection: **Thesis Outline**.
  - Reason: after the reader has the problem, justice lens, gap, and questions, they should know how the thesis is structured.

### Recommended final Chapter 1 structure
1. `MOTIVATION & PROBLEM FRAMING`
2. `CLIMATE CHANGE, FLOODS & JUSTICE`
3. `RESEARCH GAP & THESIS CONTRIBUTION`
4. `RESEARCH QUESTIONS & ANALYTICAL ORIENTATION`
5. `THESIS OUTLINE`

### Why this revised structure is stronger
- It gives the introduction a clearer narrative arc.
- It prevents the chapter from ending too abruptly with only research questions.
- It reduces overlap with Chapter 2 by keeping the justice discussion concise and introductory, not fully theoretical.
- It reduces overlap with Chapter 3 by introducing the analytical logic only in a light way, not yet in full design language.

## Overall job of Chapter 1
- Open the thesis with a strong and credible problem statement.
- Establish flood risk as a socially and politically relevant issue, not only as a hydrological one.
- Move from general flood relevance to the German and Elbe context.
- Introduce the justice lens without reproducing Chapter 2 in full.
- Identify the specific empirical and conceptual gap that this thesis addresses.
- Present the two fixed research questions exactly as they stand.
- Give the reader a short roadmap for the rest of the thesis.

## Red thread of the whole chapter
- Floods matter more and more under climate change and socio-economic development.
- Flood risk management is therefore increasingly important, but it is not socially neutral.
- This makes the distributive dimension of flood exposure and flood protection analytically relevant.
- Existing research shows that flood exposure and vulnerability can be socially differentiated, but there is still a gap in municipality-level justice-oriented evidence for the Elbe context.
- This thesis addresses that gap through a spatial comparison of flood exposure, vulnerability, and protection-related differentiation.

## One-sentence chapter logic
- Because flood risk is rising, adaptation matters, and the benefits and burdens of flood management are not socially neutral, this thesis examines whether flood exposure and protection-related outcomes are distributed unevenly across socio-economic groups in the Elbe context.

## Things Chapter 1 must not do
- Do **not** explain the full theory of justice; Chapter 2 does that.
- Do **not** explain the full conceptual triad in detail; Chapter 2 and Chapter 3 do that.
- Do **not** go deeply into data processing, GIS, PCA, or model setup; Chapter 4 does that.
- Do **not** promise that the thesis can prove intentional injustice.
- Do **not** oversell causal claims.
- Do **not** let the chapter sound like a methods memo.
- Do **not** repeat the basin/corridor distinction with the same level of detail as in Chapter 3.

## Recommended chapter rhythm
- `MOTIVATION & PROBLEM FRAMING`: 3 to 4 paragraphs
- `CLIMATE CHANGE, FLOODS & JUSTICE`: 2 to 3 paragraphs
- `RESEARCH GAP & THESIS CONTRIBUTION`: 2 to 3 paragraphs
- `RESEARCH QUESTIONS & ANALYTICAL ORIENTATION`: 1 to 2 paragraphs
- `THESIS OUTLINE`: 1 short paragraph

## How Chapter 1 should connect to later chapters
- `Chapter 2` will deepen the theoretical and conceptual discussion.
- `Chapter 3` will translate the questions into the analytical design.
- `Chapter 4` will operationalize the design in data and methods.
- Therefore, `Chapter 1` should open, orient, and motivate, but not fully explain everything.

## MOTIVATION & PROBLEM FRAMING

### Core function of this subsection
- Convince the reader that the topic matters materially, politically, and socially.
- Establish why floods are an urgent issue.
- Connect this urgency to adaptation and protection.
- End with the practical distributive puzzle that motivates the thesis.

### Internal red thread
- Floods are a major hazard globally and in Europe.
- Climate change and socio-economic development intensify future impacts.
- Germany is a highly relevant flood-governance case.
- The Elbe is an analytically strong river corridor.
- The real question is therefore not only how much flood risk exists, but how its burdens and benefits are distributed.

### Paragraph plan

#### Paragraph 1 - Why floods matter at all
**What this paragraph must do**
- Open strongly and credibly.
- Show that flooding is a major hazard, not a niche topic.

**Core claims to make**
- Floods are among the most consequential environmental hazards worldwide.
- Flood exposure has risen over time and is expected to continue increasing.
- Flood relevance is driven by both physical hazard and socio-economic concentration in flood-prone areas.

**Best sources**
- Must-have:
  - `Global exposure to river and coastal flooding: Long term trends and changes`
  - `Climate change and river floods in the European Union: Socio-economic consequences and the costs and benefits of adaptation`
- Optional supporting:
  - `Modelling the socio-economic impact of river floods in Europe`
  - `Comprehensive Assessment of Flood Socioeconomic Impacts Through Text‐Mining`

**Strong quote hooks / claims to anchor**
- `Global exposure to river and coastal flooding...`: flooding affects “several million people globally each year”.
- Same source: global exposure value rose from `USD 46 trillion` in 2010 to a projected `USD 80 trillion` by 2050.
- `Climate change and river floods in the European Union...`: expected annual damages rise sharply under a no-adaptation pathway.

**What to write in your own words**
- Interpret this as a combined problem of hydrological change and exposure concentration.
- The point is not only that floods happen, but that more people, assets, and infrastructures are in harm’s way.

**What not to do here**
- Do not start with the Elbe immediately.
- Do not start with justice vocabulary immediately.
- First establish the scale of the issue.

**Transition out**
- Move from flood relevance to the fact that societies must manage, adapt to, and govern floods.

#### Paragraph 2 - Why climate change and adaptation matter
**What this paragraph must do**
- Shift from hazard relevance to governance relevance.
- Make adaptation central early.

**Core claims to make**
- Climate change increases the urgency of adaptation and protection.
- Flood governance is now not just reactive emergency response, but a long-term adaptation issue.
- Decisions about protection become increasingly important because the stakes are growing.

**Best sources**
- Must-have:
  - `Climate change and river floods in the European Union...`
- Optional supporting:
  - `Flood risk assessments at different spatial scales`
  - `Process-Based Flood Risk Assessment for Germany`

**Strong quote hooks / claims to anchor**
- `Climate change and river floods in the European Union...`: “current levels of protection are kept constant”.
- Same source: adaptation can be highly cost-effective and can reduce future losses substantially.
- `Flood risk assessments at different spatial scales`: protection assumptions strongly affect risk estimates.

**What to write in your own words**
- Adaptation is not only about whether to act, but about how to act.
- Protection is one important adaptation response, but not the only one.

**What not to do here**
- Do not yet give the full justice argument.
- This paragraph should prepare, not complete, that move.

**Transition out**
- Move from general adaptation to the German and Elbe case, where flood governance has been highly visible and politically consequential.

#### Paragraph 3 - Why Germany and why the Elbe are a strong case
**What this paragraph must do**
- Introduce the empirical setting without repeating Chapter 2.
- Justify the case selection in one clean movement.

**Core claims to make**
- Germany is a strong case because major floods have repeatedly reshaped flood risk management.
- The Elbe is especially relevant because it combines severe flood history, long-term intervention, and policy learning.
- The Elbe is therefore not just a river basin, but a politically and analytically important flood corridor.

**Best sources**
- Must-have:
  - `Review of the flood risk management system in Germany after the major flood in 2013`
  - `Benchmarking flood risk reduction in the Elbe River`
- Optional supporting:
  - `A socio-hydrological model for the Elbe`
  - `Coping with extremes– experiences from event management during the recent Elbe flood disaster in 2013`

**Strong quote hooks / claims to anchor**
- `Review...`: 2002 marked a reorientation toward integrated flood risk management in Germany.
- `Benchmarking...`: the 2002 and 2013 Elbe floods triggered the Elbe Flood Protection Action Plan.
- `Coping with extremes...`: the 2013 event again showed the limits of technical protection.

**What to write in your own words**
- The Elbe is useful not because it is unique in every sense, but because it is a high-salience case where flood governance, protection, and exposure can be studied particularly clearly.

**Transition out**
- Move from case relevance to the social puzzle: if flood governance matters, who benefits and who remains exposed?

#### Paragraph 4 - The practical puzzle
**What this paragraph must do**
- End the subsection with the concrete empirical problem.
- Prepare the justice lens.

**Core claims to make**
- Flood impacts are socially uneven.
- Exposure, capacity to adapt, and recovery potential differ across socio-economic conditions.
- This makes the distributive pattern of exposure and protection a legitimate research problem.

**Best sources**
- Must-have:
  - `Floods, flood policies and changes in welfare and inequality: Evidence from Germany`
  - `Differential Exposure to Climate Change? Evidence from the 2021 Floods in Germany`
- Optional supporting:
  - `Poorly adapted but nothing to lose? A study on the flood risk – income relationship with a focus on low-income households`
  - `Social vulnerability to floods: Review of case studies and implications for measurement`

**Strong quote hooks / claims to anchor**
- `Floods, flood policies...`: floods affect households differently across income levels.
- Same source: welfare losses disproportionately harm low-income households.
- `Differential Exposure to Climate Change?...`: exposure to the 2021 floods was regressive.

**What to write in your own words**
- This is the exact point where the introduction should pivot from “why floods matter” to “why justice matters”.
- The question is no longer only whether flood risk exists, but how flood-related burdens and benefits are socially distributed.

**Transition to next subsection**
- End with a sentence such as: once adaptation and protection are socially differentiated, flood governance becomes a justice issue as well as a risk-management issue.

## CLIMATE CHANGE, FLOODS & JUSTICE

### Core function of this subsection
- Introduce the justice lens early, but in a compact way.
- Explain why flood adaptation and protection are not socially neutral.
- Establish distributive justice as the main lens of the thesis.

### Internal red thread
- Adaptation decisions distribute risk reduction and remaining burdens.
- Flood protection can redistribute safety, exposure, and residual risk.
- Therefore, justice is intrinsic to flood risk management.
- This thesis focuses mainly on the distributive dimension of that problem.

### Paragraph plan

#### Paragraph 1 - Why adaptation is also a justice issue
**What this paragraph must do**
- Link climate adaptation directly to fairness.

**Core claims to make**
- Climate adaptation always involves allocation decisions.
- Flood risk management therefore has a distributional dimension.
- The key question is not only whether strategies are effective, but for whom they are effective.

**Best sources**
- Must-have:
  - `Fair strategies to tackle unfair risks? Justice considerations within flood risk management`
  - `Justice and flood risk management: reflecting on different approaches to distribute and allocate flood risk management in Europe`
  - `Environmental justice and redistribution`
- Optional supporting:
  - `Does the environmental inequality matter? A literature review`
  - `IS INEQUALITY BAD FOR THE ENVIRONMENT?`

**Strong quote hooks / claims to anchor**
- `Fair strategies...`: justice in FRM concerns distributive and procedural questions.
- `Justice and flood risk management...`: flood protection involves decisions about who or what should be protected.
- `Environmental justice and redistribution`: flood prevention can lead to environmental injustice.

**What to write in your own words**
- The introduction only needs a light-touch version of this argument.
- Save the deeper normative discussion for Chapter 2.

**Transition out**
- Move from adaptation justice in general to flood-specific distributive injustice.

#### Paragraph 2 - Flood governance is not socially neutral
**What this paragraph must do**
- Show that flood management can create uneven outcomes.

**Core claims to make**
- Flood protection does not simply eliminate risk; it can redistribute risk and benefits.
- Uneven outcomes may arise both from physical geography and from public interventions.
- The thesis focuses mainly on distributive patterns, even if procedural and recognition issues matter too.

**Best sources**
- Must-have:
  - `Environmental justice and redistribution`
  - `Natural and imposed injustices: the challenges in implementing ‘fair’ flood risk management policy in England`
  - `Justice and flood risk management...`
- Optional supporting:
  - `Beyond‘just’ flood risk management: the potential for—and limits to—alleviating flood disadvantage`
  - `Modeling the Impacts of Urban Flood Risk Management on Social Inequality`

**Strong quote hooks / claims to anchor**
- `Natural and imposed injustices...`: flooding is “not fair per se”.
- `Environmental justice and redistribution`: unjust floodwater redistribution.
- `Beyond ‘just’ flood risk management...`: flood disadvantage extends beyond one isolated hazard event.

**What to write in your own words**
- Make clear that unequal outcomes do not automatically prove deliberate injustice.
- They do, however, create a justice-relevant empirical pattern worth examining.

**Transition out**
- Move from normative relevance to empirical necessity: if these patterns exist, how are they studied and what is missing in the literature?

### Mini-checklist
- Did you introduce justice early but not too heavily?
- Did you define the thesis focus as primarily distributive?
- Did you avoid reproducing Chapter 2 in miniature?

## RESEARCH GAP & THESIS CONTRIBUTION

### Core function of this subsection
- Identify the academic gap precisely.
- Distinguish the thesis from purely hydrological, engineering, or governance studies.
- State the thesis contribution clearly and modestly.

### Internal red thread
- There is substantial flood-risk research.
- There is also growing work on environmental inequality and flood justice.
- But municipality-level evidence linking exposure, vulnerability, and protection-related differentiation remains limited, especially in the German and Elbe context.
- This thesis contributes exactly there.

### Paragraph plan

#### Paragraph 1 - What the literature already does well
**What this paragraph must do**
- Avoid a fake gap.
- Show respect for existing research before identifying what is missing.

**Core claims to make**
- The flood literature already offers strong work on hazard, damages, flood risk management, and social vulnerability.
- International studies increasingly examine social disparities in flood exposure.
- German research has also begun to document differential exposure and vulnerability.

**Best sources**
- Must-have:
  - `Environmental Inequalities in Flood Exposure: A Matter of Scale`
  - `Disparities of population exposed to flood hazards in the United States`
  - `Social disparities in neighborhood flood exposure in 44,698 urban neighborhoods in Latin America`
  - `Differential Exposure to Climate Change? Evidence from the 2021 Floods in Germany`
- Optional supporting:
  - `Inequalities in exposure and awareness of flood risk in England and Wales`
  - `Assessment of Social Vulnerability for River-Floods in Germany`
  - `Validation of a social vulnerability index in context to river-floods in Germany`

**Strong quote hooks / claims to anchor**
- `Environmental Inequalities in Flood Exposure...`: exposure varies by scale.
- `Differential Exposure to Climate Change?...`: exposure was regressive at the local level in Germany.
- `Assessment of Social Vulnerability for River-Floods in Germany`: an overview of social vulnerability patterns in Germany was missing.

**What to write in your own words**
- Say clearly that the field is not empty.
- The point is not that nobody studied flood inequality, but that the combination you need is still underdeveloped.

**Transition out**
- Move from what exists to what still remains unresolved.

#### Paragraph 2 - What is still missing
**What this paragraph must do**
- Name the actual gap without exaggeration.

**Core claims to make**
- Existing work often focuses on one of three things in isolation:
  - physical hazard and damage,
  - social vulnerability measurement,
  - or governance and protection strategies.
- Fewer studies link these dimensions in a municipality-level distributive framework for the Elbe context.
- German evidence is improving, but still limited at the specific scale and framing of this thesis.

**Best sources**
- Must-have:
  - `Environmental Inequalities in Flood Exposure: A Matter of Scale`
  - `How Valid Are Social Vulnerability Models?`
  - `Social vulnerability to floods: Review of case studies and implications for measurement`

  - `Uncertainty Analysis for a Social Vulnerability Index`
  - `Modeling the Impacts of Urban Flood Risk Management on Social Inequality`

**Strong quote hooks / claims to anchor**
- `How Valid Are Social Vulnerability Models?`: social vulnerability models are mainly descriptive.
- `Social vulnerability to floods...`: there is a gap between contextual complexity and generalized indicator practice.
- `Validation of a social vulnerability index in context to river-floods in Germany`: there was a gap in comprehensive subnational vulnerability mapping for German river corridors.

**What to write in your own words**
- Your gap is not “nobody has ever studied floods and inequality”.
- Your gap is: a municipality-level, justice-oriented linkage of flood exposure and socio-economic vulnerability in the Elbe corridor, with protection kept conceptually central.

**Transition out**
- Move from gap to explicit contribution.

#### Paragraph 3 - What this thesis contributes
**What this paragraph must do**

**Core claims to make**
- The thesis contributes a municipality-based distributive analysis in the Elbe context.
- It links multi-return-period flood exposure to socio-economic vulnerability.
- It keeps protection conceptually central while handling it empirically with caution.
- It therefore contributes both to the German flood literature and to the broader flood-justice debate.

**Best sources**

## THESIS OUTLINE

### Core function of this subsection
- Close the introduction cleanly.
- Give the reader a roadmap through the thesis.

### Why this subsection should be added
- Without a chapter outline, the introduction ends slightly abruptly.
- With a chapter outline, the reader knows where the theory, design, methods, and results will appear.
- This improves flow and makes the thesis feel more deliberate.

### What should go in
- 1 short paragraph, maximum 5 to 6 sentences.
- Sentence 1: Chapter 2 provides the theoretical and conceptual background.
- Sentence 2: Chapter 3 presents the research design and analytical framework.
- Sentence 3: Chapter 4 introduces the data and methods.
- Sentence 4: Chapter 5 presents the empirical results.
- Sentence 5: Chapter 6 discusses the findings and their implications.
- Sentence 6: Chapter 7 concludes.

### What not to do here
- Do not summarize every chapter in detail.
- Do not start discussing findings.
- Keep it neat and short.
